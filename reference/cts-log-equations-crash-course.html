Log Equations, Spelled Out

The dummy-proof crash course: what a logarithm is, why the decibel uses one, when to use 10-log vs 20-log, and every worked example with nothing skipped.

1 · Why logs exist at all

Your ears can hear everything from a mosquito across the room to a jet engine. The pressure difference between the quietest thing you can hear and the threshold of pain is about 3,000,000 : 1. If we measured sound on a normal (linear) scale, we'd be writing numbers like 0.00002 Pa and 60 Pa in the same sentence. Ugly, error-prone, and — worse — it doesn't match how you hear.

Your hearing doesn't perceive loudness linearly. Going from 1 watt to 2 watts sounds like the same size of jump as going from 100 watts to 200 watts. What your ear cares about is the ratio, not the raw difference. Logarithms are the math tool that turns ratios into simple, small numbers.

KEY TERM

Logarithmic perception: humans perceive sound level differences by ratio (multiplication), not by amount (addition). That's why the entire audio world runs on a base-10 log scale.

2 · What a log actually is

Here is the entire concept in one sentence:

log(x) = "how many times do I multiply 10 by itself to get x?"In AV, "log" always means log base 10 (log₁₀). Your calculator's LOG button. Not LN.

That's it. A log is a counting machine for zeros (and, more precisely, for factors of 10):

  • log(1,000) = 3  because 10 × 10 × 10 = 1,000. You multiplied 10 by itself 3 times.
  • log(100) = 2  because 10 × 10 = 100.
  • log(10) = 1  because 10 by itself is… 10.
  • log(1) = 0  because you multiplied 10 by itself zero times. Nothing changed.
  • log(0.001) = −3  because you divided by 10 three times. Going below 1 makes logs negative.
WATCH OUT

A negative log does not mean a negative quantity. log(0.5) = −0.301 just means "0.5 is a bit less than one factor-of-10 below 1." In dB terms, negative = loss, positive = gain. Never a negative voltage or negative sound.

3 · The logs you'll actually meet

Exam questions are built so the log values land on a handful of "landmark" numbers. Memorize these four and you can sanity-check almost any dB calculation without a calculator:

EXAM TIP

The sign-flip rule is a free answer-checker: if the ratio inside the log is less than 1 (measured value smaller than reference, or listener farther than the reference distance), the dB result must be negative — a loss. Greater than 1 → positive → gain. If your sign doesn't match the story, you flipped the fraction.

4 · From logs to decibels

A decibel is not a unit of loudness. It's a unit of comparison. Every dB figure is secretly a sentence: "this thing is X compared to that thing." The original unit was the Bel (after Alexander Graham Bell):

Bel = log(quantity₁ / quantity₂)

The Bel turned out to be too coarse for real work, so the industry uses tenths of a Bel — decibels. Ten decibels per Bel means we multiply by 10:

dB = 10 × log(quantity₁ / quantity₂)1 dB = 0.1 Bel — that's the whole origin of the "10 ×" out front.
KEY TERM

Decibel (dB): the logarithmic ratio between two quantities of the same physical property (power, voltage, pressure, distance). Because it's a ratio, a dB value is meaningless without knowing what it's compared against — that's why reference levels (Section 9) exist.

WATCH OUT

Saying "the room is at 85 dB" only means something because there's an implied reference: 85 dB SPL, referenced to the threshold of hearing. "It's 10 degrees hotter than yesterday" tells you nothing unless you know yesterday's temperature. Same logic.

5 · The one decision: 10-log or 20-log?

This is where everyone gets tripped up, so let's kill it permanently. There is exactly one question to ask:

"Am I comparing watts?"

  • Yes — it's power (watts): use 10 log.
  • No — it's voltage, distance, or sound pressure: use 20 log.

Why 20 and not 10? (The actual reason)

Power is related to the square of voltage: P = V² / R. Double the voltage → four times the power. So when you compare voltages, you're implicitly comparing squared quantities. In log-world, squaring a number doubles its log — that's a core log rule: log(x²) = 2 × log(x). The doubled log turns the 10 into a 20:

10 × log(V₁²/V₂²)  =  10 × 2 × log(V₁/V₂)  =  20 × log(V₁/V₂)

Distance works the same way for a different reason: radiated energy (sound, light, RF) spreads over an area that grows with the square of distance — the inverse square law. Square relationship → doubled log → 20.

EXAM TIP

Mnemonic: "Power stands alone" — power gets the plain 10. Voltage, distance, and pressure all square up to become power, so they get double: 20.

6 · The three exam formulas

All three are the same machine wearing different hats. Same shape every time: a coefficient, a log, a ratio.

dB = 10 × log(P₁ / P₂)POWER — P₁ = new/measured power, P₂ = reference power (both in watts)
dB = 20 × log(V₁ / Vᵣ)VOLTAGE — V₁ = new/measured voltage, Vᵣ = reference voltage
dB = 20 × log(D₁ / D₂)DISTANCE (inverse square law) — D₁ = original/reference distance, D₂ = new/measured distance
WATCH OUT

Notice the distance formula puts the reference distance on top (D₁ = original, D₂ = new) — opposite of the voltage and power formulas, where the new value is on top. This is how AVIXA writes them, and exam answer options are built around it. Moving away from a loudspeaker: farther distance goes on the bottom, ratio < 1, result negative = loss. That matches physical reality — use the sign check from Section 3.

7 · Worked examples — every single step

Each example below reveals one step at a time. Try to predict the next step before you tap the button. That's the whole trick to making this stick.

Example 1 — Doubling the distance
A listener moves from 1 m to 2 m away from a loudspeaker. What's the change in SPL?
STEP 1 — PICK THE FORMULA

We're comparing distances, not watts → 20-log. Distance formula: reference distance on top.

dB = 20 × log(D₁ / D₂)
STEP 2 — PLUG IN THE NUMBERS

D₁ = 1 m (original), D₂ = 2 m (new position).

dB = 20 × log(1 / 2)
STEP 3 — DO THE DIVISION FIRST

Always resolve the fraction before touching the log. 1 ÷ 2 = 0.5.

dB = 20 × log(0.5)
STEP 4 — TAKE THE LOG

log(0.5) = −0.301 (landmark value: halving = −0.3). Negative because 0.5 < 1.

dB = 20 × (−0.301)
STEP 5 — MULTIPLYdB = −6.02 ≈ −6 dB
ANSWER + SANITY CHECK

−6 dB. Negative = loss, which makes sense — we moved away. This is the famous 6 dB per doubling rule. Every doubling of distance costs you another 6 dB.

Example 2 — The farthest listener (the classic exam setup)
A loudspeaker is rated 92 dB SPL, 1 watt @ 1 meter. The farthest listener sits 8 m away. What SPL do they get with 1 watt in?
STEP 1 — UNDERSTAND THE SENSITIVITY RATING

"92 dB SPL, 1 W @ 1 m" means: feed it 1 watt, stand 1 meter away, you measure 92 dB SPL. That's our anchor point. Now we need the loss from 1 m out to 8 m.

STEP 2 — PICK THE FORMULA

Comparing distances → 20-log, reference distance (1 m) on top.

dB = 20 × log(1 / 8)
STEP 3 — DIVIDEdB = 20 × log(0.125)
STEP 4 — TAKE THE LOG

log(0.125) = −0.903. No calculator needed: 8 = 2×2×2, so log(8) = 0.3+0.3+0.3 = 0.903, and 1/8 flips the sign.

dB = 20 × (−0.903)
STEP 5 — MULTIPLYdB = −18.06 ≈ −18 dB
STEP 6 — APPLY THE LOSS TO THE RATING

Start at the anchor (92 dB @ 1 m) and subtract the distance loss.

92 dB − 18 dB = 74 dB SPL
ANSWER + SANITY CHECK

74 dB SPL at the farthest listener. Shortcut check: 1→2 m (−6), 2→4 m (−6), 4→8 m (−6). Three doublings × 6 dB = −18 dB. Same answer, zero calculator.

Example 3 — Solving for power (working the formula backwards)
Continuing Example 2: the design target at the listener is 80 dB SPL (70 dB speech + 10 dB headroom), but 1 watt only gets us 74 dB. We're 6 dB short. How many watts do we need?
STEP 1 — PICK THE FORMULA

Now we're comparing watts → 10-log.

SPL increase = 10 × log(P₂ / P₁)
STEP 2 — PLUG IN WHAT WE KNOW

The increase we need is 6 dB. The reference power P₁ is 1 W (from the sensitivity rating). The unknown is x.

6 = 10 × log(x / 1)
STEP 3 — ISOLATE THE LOG

Divide both sides by 10 to get the log alone.

0.6 = log(x)
STEP 4 — UNDO THE LOG

The opposite of "log" is "10 to the power of." Raise 10 to both sides. (Calculator: the 10ˣ button, usually SHIFT + LOG.)

x = 10^0.6
STEP 5 — COMPUTEx = 3.98 watts ≈ 4 W
ANSWER + SANITY CHECK

About 4 watts. Check with magic numbers: +3 dB = double the power. We need +6 dB = two doublings = 1 W → 2 W → 4 W. ✔ Also notice: quadrupling the power only bought us 6 dB. Power is expensive; dB are stubborn.

Example 4 — Voltage gain
A line driver takes a 0.775 V input and outputs 7.75 V. What's the gain in dB?
STEP 1 — PICK THE FORMULA

Volts → 20-log. New/measured value on top, reference on the bottom.

dB = 20 × log(V₁ / Vᵣ)
STEP 2 — PLUG INdB = 20 × log(7.75 / 0.775)
STEP 3 — DIVIDE

7.75 ÷ 0.775 = 10. The output is ten times the input voltage.

dB = 20 × log(10)
STEP 4 — TAKE THE LOG

log(10) = 1. One factor of ten.

dB = 20 × 1
ANSWER + SANITY CHECK

+20 dB. Positive = gain ✔. Burn this in: 10× the voltage = +20 dB, but 10× the power = +10 dB. Same ratio, different coefficient, different answer — exactly what the exam loves to test.

Example 5 — Doubling amplifier power
You swap a 100 W amp for a 200 W amp. How much more level do you get?
STEP 1 — PICK THE FORMULA

Watts → 10-log.

dB = 10 × log(P₁ / P₂)
STEP 2 — PLUG INdB = 10 × log(200 / 100)
STEP 3 — DIVIDEdB = 10 × log(2)
STEP 4 — TAKE THE LOG

log(2) = 0.301. The most important landmark log there is.

dB = 10 × 0.301
ANSWER + SANITY CHECK

+3 dB. Double the power = +3 dB, and 3 dB is only a "just noticeable" change to human ears. You doubled your amp budget for a just-noticeable difference. (To sound twice as loud you'd need +10 dB = ten times the power.)

8 · Going backwards: dB → ratio

Half the exam questions run the machine in reverse: "you gained 6 dB — what happened to the power?" The undo button for a log is 10 raised to a power (the antilog):

Power ratio = 10^(dB ÷ 10)watts: divide the dB by 10 first, then hit 10ˣ
Voltage / distance / pressure ratio = 10^(dB ÷ 20)everything else: divide by 20 first, then 10ˣ

It's the forward process mirror-imaged. Forward: divide → log → multiply by the coefficient. Backward: divide by the coefficient → 10ˣ → that's your ratio.

Quick example: +6 dB of power gain. 6 ÷ 10 = 0.6. Then 10^0.6 = 3.98 ≈ 4. The power quadrupled — exactly what Example 3 found the long way.

WATCH OUT

Divide by the coefficient before raising 10 to the power. Computing 10^6 and then dividing gives garbage. The dB has to be shrunk back down to a bare log value first.

9 · Reference levels — the "compared to what?" cheat sheet

A dB with a letter after it is just a dB with the reference baked in. Memorize the reference value for each:

Notation0 dB means…MeasuresUses
dB SPL20 µPaSound pressure20 log — threshold of human hearing
dBu0.775 V (775 mV)Voltage20 log — pro audio line level
dBV1.0 VVoltage20 log — consumer line level
dBm1 mWPower10 log — electrical power
SPEC

0 dB SPL = 20 microPascals — the quietest sound a human can hear. 1 Pa = 0.00015 psi, so this is an absurdly tiny pressure. Also note −20 dBu is not a negative voltage — it's simply a voltage smaller than 775 mV.

EXAM TIP

Coming from audio, you already know dBu vs dBV from gear spec sheets: +4 dBu (pro) vs −10 dBV (consumer). The exam only needs you to match each suffix to its reference value and know whether it's a 10-log (power: dBm) or 20-log (voltage/pressure: dBu, dBV, dB SPL) family member.

10 · The magic numbers (tap to flip)

These are the values that let you answer most dB questions without a calculator. Tap each card.

+6 dB
Double the voltage — or halve the distance to the source.
−6 dB
Doubling of distance. The inverse square law's per-doubling price.
+10 dB
10× the power. Perceived as twice as loud by humans.
+20 dB

EXAM TIP

Trap pairing to keep straight: 3 dB = double the power (physics), but 10 dB = double the loudness (perception). Different questions, different answers. Read whether the question asks about watts or about what a listener hears.

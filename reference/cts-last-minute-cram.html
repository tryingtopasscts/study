The Last Mile

Everything that isn't the Core Five. Built from a passing candidate's five essentials and checked against AVIXA's exam blueprint — read this the night before, not the month before.

The five essentials

One candidate's post-exam debrief, reproduced in full. It is the spine of this page.

"I just passed the basic CTS exam. If I could boil down what you must know to 5 essential topics, it would be:

1. Number one, and most importantly, is reading comprehension of multiple choice questions. I'll also throw in graphs and reflected ceiling plans, but less so (do the practice exams!!).
2. Know the basic math and formulas for calculating volume, ohms law, aspect ratio, throw distance, SPL/light change over distance (inverse sq law).
3. Know all cable types, including their wiring, impedance, signal type, and communication protocol.
4. Know the order and purpose of all events and milestones that take place on an AV project from conception to termination (emphasis on site visits).
5. If an answer mentions an 'end user', that is likely the correct answer.

I know #2 is kind of cheating with multiple topics but seriously, if you have a middle-school level understanding of math, all you need is to memorize a few formulas, and then 10–20% of the questions are freebies." — r/CommercialAV, post-exam debrief
How To Use This Page Essential #2 has its own dedicated page — it's the only one that needs drilling rather than reading. The other four are recognition knowledge: you need to have seen them recently, not to have practised them. That's why this is a last-pass document. Work top to bottom, then take the drill at the bottom.
Where This Comes From The quote above is one person's experience, not an official source. Where it lines up with AVIXA's published Exam Content Outline and Job Task Analysis, this page says so. Where it's a heuristic rather than a fact — #5 especially — this page flags it. Treat the blueprint as evidence and the debrief as a study aid.
01
The #1 Essential · Applies to all 100 items

Read The Question

The candidate ranked this above the math, and the blueprint quietly agrees — "basic math" appears as a skill, while the knowledge items are about interpreting requirements, drawings and documentation.

In Plain English CTS items are rarely "do you know this fact." They're "given this situation, what's the best action." That means several answers will be true statements, and only one answers the question that was actually asked. Most missed questions are comprehension failures wearing a knowledge-gap costume.

The qualifier words that change everything

Word in the stemWhat it does to the answer
BEST / MOSTSeveral options are acceptable. Rank them — you want the optimum, not merely a workable one.
FIRST / NEXTSequence question. The answer is usually the information-gathering or verification step, not the fix.
EXCEPT / NOT / LEASTYou are hunting the odd one out. Three options will be correct. Flip your thinking deliberately.
PRIMARY / MAINSecondary benefits will be offered as bait. Ask what the thing exists for.
ALWAYS / NEVER / ALL / ONLYIn an answer, absolutes are usually wrong — AV has too many exceptions.
MINIMUM / MAXIMUMA spec question. There's a number, and the distractors bracket it.

How the wrong answers are built

Distractors on a well-written exam aren't random. Recognising the pattern often gets you to the answer without knowing the fact:

The near-twin

The correct term's partner — resistance vs. impedance, margin vs. markup, estimate vs. quote. If two options are a known pair, one of them is the answer.

The skipped step

In math items, the result you'd get by omitting a conversion — diagonal used instead of width, feet left as inches.

The right fact, wrong question

A true statement that doesn't answer what was asked. Very common on "BEST" and "PRIMARY" items.

The unilateral action

Something a technician might actually do, but without consulting, documenting, or verifying first. Almost always wrong on this exam.

The Two-Pass Method Pass 1: answer everything you're sure of, and flag the rest. Do not burn four minutes on question seven. Pass 2: return to flagged items with the clock pressure gone and the rest of the exam's vocabulary now fresh in your head — later questions frequently jog the answer to earlier ones. With 100 items, you have roughly 1.5 minutes each; the ones you know take 20 seconds, which is what funds the hard ones.
Your Own Data Says This Your logged diagnostics put 44% of misses in "term confusions" — both things known, pairing wrong — and another 4% in outright reading slips. Neither is a study gap. Both are fixed at the moment of reading, by slowing down on the stem and naming which pair is being tested before you look at the options.

Cable & Connectivity

Four attributes per cable: wiring · impedance · signal type · protocol. That's the framing the debrief used, and it's the right one — exam items almost always test one attribute against another cable's.

The impedance table — memorise this block

If you learn one table tonight, this is it. Impedance is the single most testable cable attribute because every value belongs to exactly one family.

ImpedanceBelongs toTypical cable & connector
50 ΩRF — antennas, wireless mic distributionRG-58 · BNC / N-type
75 ΩVideo coax, SDI, S/PDIF, AES3idRG-6 / RG-59 / RG-11 · BNC, F-type, RCA
100 ΩBalanced twisted pair — network & AV-over-IPCat5e / Cat6 / Cat6A · RJ-45
110 ΩAES3 (AES/EBU) professional digital audioShielded twisted pair · XLR-3
120 ΩRS-485 and DMX512 termination resistorAt both ends of the chain
600 ΩLegacy analog audio line matchingHistorical — modern gear is bridging, not matched
The 75 / 110 Near-Twin Both carry digital audio. AES3 proper is 110 Ω on shielded twisted pair with XLR. AES3id is the 75 Ω coax-and-BNC variant, which runs much further. If a question pairs an impedance with a connector, the connector is the tell: XLR → 110 Ω, BNC → 75 Ω.

Balanced vs. unbalanced — the concept behind half the audio questions

Balanced means two signal conductors carrying the same signal in opposite polarity, plus a shield. Interference hits both equally (common mode); the differential receiver subtracts one from the other, the signal survives and the noise cancels. This is common-mode rejection.
XLR Pinout — Worth Knowing Cold Pin 1 = shield/ground · Pin 2 = hot (+) · Pin 3 = cold (−). "Pin 2 hot" is the AES standard. A cable wired pin 2 to pin 3 is a polarity-inverting cable, which is a classic troubleshooting-question answer for a system where two sources cancel.
Your Own Logged Trap Your notes already flag this one: use balanced cable for everything, not just past some length threshold. "Balanced only over X metres" is a distractor. Also: coax is unbalanced by construction; noise rejection comes from twisted pair in a balanced circuit, while a shield provides physical/electrostatic protection rather than common-mode rejection.

Mic vs. Line vs. Speaker Three levels, three orders of magnitude apart. Plugging a mic into a line input gives you a signal too quiet to use; the reverse overloads and distorts. Speaker cable is unshielded because the signal is so hot that induced noise is irrelevant — and heavy-gauge because it carries real current.

Why AV cares: fibre is immune to EMI/RFI, carries no current so it breaks ground loops, and has enormous bandwidth. The trade is that it can't carry power and termination is a skilled job.

Control protocols — the ones with numbers attached

ProtocolKey numbersCharacter

RS-422~1,200 m (4,000 ft)Balanced/differential — same idea as balanced audio, so it goes much further.

The RS-485 Cluster 32 devices, 120 Ω termination at both ends, DMX512 rides on it. These three facts travel together and your weak-spots log already flags them. If a question mentions any one, the others are likely distractor material.

Twisted pair specifics

  • The 100 m channel — 90 m of solid-core horizontal cable + 10 m of stranded patch cords, total 100 m. Exceeding it is a classic "why is this link failing" answer.
  • T568A vs. T568B — the two pinout standards, differing by a swap of the green and orange pairs. Same standard at both ends = straight-through. Different at each end = crossover. Pick one standard per site and stay consistent.
  • Category — Cat5e, Cat6, Cat6A. Higher category = more bandwidth and tighter crosstalk control, not magically more distance.
  • Solid vs. stranded — solid core for permanent in-wall runs, stranded for flexible patch cords. Using stranded for a long horizontal run increases attenuation.

Fire rating — where the cable is allowed to go

RatingWhere it goes
CMP — PlenumAir-handling spaces (a ceiling void used as return air). Low smoke, flame-retardant. The most restrictive space, the most expensive cable.
CMR — RiserVertical runs between floors. Resists carrying flame floor to floor.
CM / CMG — GeneralGeneral purpose, single floor, non-plenum.

The hierarchy runs downward: plenum cable may substitute for riser or general, but never the reverse. Using non-plenum cable in a plenum space is a code violation, and it's a favourite exam scenario.

Installation numbers already in your weak-spots log

SpecValue

Cable Separation Keep AV signal cable away from mains power to avoid induced hum. Where a crossing is unavoidable, cross at 90° — perpendicular crossing minimises coupling. Running parallel alongside power for any distance is the wrong answer.

Domain A is 61% of the exam

Project Order & Milestones

Conception to termination, in order, with the purpose of each step — and the site visits the debrief singled out.

Why This Is Worth Real Time Domain A — Creating AV Solutions — is 61 of the 100 items. Its nine tasks are literally the project lifecycle in order: site survey, gather customer information, evaluate the environment, develop scope, design, sell, select vendors, provide the solution. Knowing the sequence isn't one question's worth of value; it's the organising structure of the majority of the exam.
Phase 1

Program Phase — figure out what's actually needed

Before any equipment is chosen. Conduct the needs analysis, run the site survey, understand the client's tasks and the room's constraints. Ends when everyone agrees on what the system must do.

Deliverable: Program Report — the agreed statement of need
Phase 2

Design Phase — decide how to deliver it

Schematic design → design development → construction documents. Signal flow diagrams, equipment selection, drawings, specifications, bill of materials. Coordination with architect, electrical, structural and IT happens here.

Deliverable: drawings, specs, BOM, bid documents
Phase 3

Build / Construction Phase — make it exist

Procurement, rack fabrication, rough-in (conduit, back boxes, cable pulls — before walls close), then trim-out (devices, plates, mounts) and termination. Change orders and RFIs live here.

Deliverable: an installed, terminated system
Phase 4

Verification — prove it does what was promised

System verification and commissioning, tested against the Program Report — that's the whole point of writing it down in Phase 1. Punch list of deficiencies gets generated and cleared.

Deliverable: verification checklist, punch list, as-built drawings
Phase 5

Handover / Closeout — transfer ownership

Training the end users, delivering documentation and as-builts, final client sign-off and acceptance. The warranty period starts here, not at installation.

Deliverable: training, manuals, as-builts, signed acceptance
Phase 6

Service & Maintenance — the long tail

Preventive maintenance, service contracts, first-line troubleshooting, consumables and lamp life, eventual refresh. Domains B and D live here — 28% of the exam between them.

Deliverable: maintenance logs, service records
The Loop That Ties It Together The Program Report is written in Phase 1 and is the document you verify against in Phase 4. If an exam question asks what commissioning is tested against, or why the needs analysis matters, that's the loop being tested. Design and build are the middle; the two ends talk to each other.

Site visits — the emphasis the debrief called out

The blueprint devotes two separate tasks to this (Task 1: conduct site survey; Task 4: maintain awareness of changes to the site environment). The second one matters — a site isn't surveyed once and forgotten.

Visit 1 · Program Phase

The initial site survey

Capture what's actually there: dimensions and ceiling height, existing power and data, HVAC, lighting (ambient and controllable), acoustics and finishes, structure and hanging points, sightlines, access and delivery routes, and any heritage or code constraints.

Skills named in the blueprint: measuring · conversions · calculating areas and volumes · reading blueprints · recording data
Visit 2 · Design Phase

Coordination visits

Verify design assumptions against reality and coordinate with other trades before documents are issued. Catching a conflict here costs a drawing revision; catching it in Phase 3 costs a change order.

Visit 3 · Before mobilising

The site-readiness check

Is rough-in complete? Power and data in place? Room secure, clean, climate-controlled? Sending a crew to a site that isn't ready is the expensive mistake this visit exists to prevent.

Visit 4 · Ongoing

Maintaining awareness of change

Blueprint Task 4, verbatim: furniture layouts move, finishes change, lighting gets redesigned, walls relocate. Any of those invalidates acoustic, sightline or mounting decisions already made.

Heuristic — a tiebreaker, not a fact

The End-User Rule

"If an answer mentions an end user, that is likely the correct answer." It works — and it's worth understanding why, because the underlying logic covers far more questions than the literal phrase does.

Why It Works AVIXA's entire framework is built around solving a client's problem, not deploying technology. The needs analysis exists to find out what users must accomplish. Commissioning verifies against agreed needs. Handover includes training the people who'll actually use it. An answer oriented toward the end user is aligned with the philosophy the exam is written from — so it wins by construction, not by coincidence.

The full heuristic stack, strongest first

Safety beats everything

If one option protects people and another protects schedule, budget, or equipment, safety wins. No exceptions, no qualifiers. This outranks even the end-user rule.

Serve the end user / client need

Options mentioning the end user, their requirements, or their intended task are usually right. Options that optimise for the integrator's convenience usually aren't.

Communicate, consult, document

Answers involving notifying the client, consulting a stakeholder, issuing an RFI, or recording the outcome beat answers where someone acts alone and tells nobody.

Verify before you act

On FIRST/NEXT questions, gathering information — measure it, test it, confirm the symptom, check the documentation — almost always precedes the fix.

Follow the standard or the manufacturer

Published standards, code, and manufacturer specifications beat field improvisation and rules of thumb.

Distrust absolutes

An answer containing always, never, all, only, or every is usually wrong. AV has too many legitimate exceptions for an absolute to survive.

Two answers saying the same thing are both wrong

Only one option can be correct, so if two are functionally identical, neither is it. This can eliminate half the list instantly.

Use This Honestly These are tiebreakers for questions you genuinely can't answer, not a strategy for the exam as a whole. A well-written item will happily offer an end-user-flavoured distractor to catch people pattern-matching instead of reading. Work the question first; reach for the heuristic only when you're down to two options and out of knowledge. On a 100-item exam, converting even six coin-flips into five correct answers is real — but it won't carry you past a topic you never studied.
The Sanity Check Before locking in a heuristic-driven answer, ask: "does this actually answer the question that was asked?" The most common way the end-user rule fails is a true, client-friendly statement that addresses a different question than the stem.

Reading Drawings

The debrief's "graphs and reflected ceiling plans" — the visual literacy half of Essential #1.

The four views, and which is which

The reflected ceiling plan is the one that trips people up. You are looking down, as in a floor plan, but seeing the ceiling — as though it were reflected in a mirror on the floor. Orientation therefore matches the floor plan exactly, which is what lets you coordinate a ceiling speaker against the seat below it.
Why AV Lives On The RCP Ceiling loudspeakers, projector mounts, lighting fixtures, HVAC diffusers, sprinkler heads, access panels and ceiling tile grid all appear on the RCP. It's where AV coordination conflicts are found — a ceiling speaker and a sprinkler head wanting the same tile. If a question involves placing anything overhead, the RCP is the drawing.

Abbreviations that appear on drawings

Abbr.Meaning
AFFAbove Finished Floor — a mounting height
NTSNot To Scale — you cannot measure it; ask instead
NICNot In Contract — shown for context, someone else's scope
FUTFuture — planned for a later phase
OFE / OFCIOwner Furnished Equipment / Owner Furnished, Contractor Installed
TYPTypical — this detail repeats wherever the condition occurs
Scale — The Direction Matters A drawing marked 1:50 means one unit on paper equals 50 in reality, so you multiply the measured dimension by 50. Dividing by mistake is a factor-of-2,500 error on an area calculation. And if the drawing says NTS, no measurement off it is valid at all — recognising that is the answer to some questions.

Other drawing conventions worth recognising: solid lines are visible edges, dashed lines are hidden or above the cut plane, the title block carries scale/date/revision, a revision cloud marks what changed, and the north arrow orients the plan.

Formulas Beyond The Five

Lower-yield than the Core Five, but cheap to carry. Each is one line.

Heat load

WhatFormula
Equipment heat outputBTU/hr = Watts × 3.41

Every watt a device consumes ends up as heat in the room. Rack heat load drives HVAC sizing, and "the rack is overheating" troubleshooting questions trace back to it.

Projector brightness

Conceptually: lumens needed = screen area × the illuminance you need on it, adjusted for screen gain and projector derating. The full AVIXA version with contrast ratio targets is on the CTS-D formula sheet — for CTS, know the relationship: bigger screen or brighter room means more lumens, and controlling ambient light is cheaper than buying brightness.

Money math — Domain C is 11% of the exam

WhatFormula
Markup %(Price − Cost) ÷ Cost
Margin %(Price − Cost) ÷ Price
Margin ≠ Markup The denominator is the entire difference, and it's a guaranteed near-twin distractor. A $100 item sold at $150 carries a 50% markup but only a 33% margin. If a question gives you one and asks for the other, that gap is exactly what's being tested.

Decibel quick numbers

ChangeMeans
+3 dBDouble the power (watts)
+6 dBDouble the voltage or sound pressure — or halve the distance
+10 dB10× the power ≈ twice as loud to a listener
−6 dBDouble the distance from a point source

The split that matters: 10 log for power, 20 log for pressure, voltage and distance.

Final Checklist

The last hour, and the exam room itself.

The night before — read, don't drill
  • The impedance table — 50 / 75 / 100 / 110 / 120 Ω and who owns each
  • The five phases in order, and that verification tests against the Program Report
  • The scratch-board dump — write it out once by hand, triangles included
  • The qualifier words — BEST, FIRST, EXCEPT, PRIMARY
  • The heuristic stack — safety, end user, communicate, verify
  • Your weak-spots log — the term confusions, since they're 44% of your misses
  • Then stop. Sleep is worth more than one more pass.
In The Exam Room You may not bring anything in — no formula sheet, no calculator. An on-screen calculator is provided, and the math never goes beyond add, subtract, multiply, divide, square, square root and log. 100 questions. Dump the scratch-board lines before question one. Flag and move on rather than grinding. Answer every item — there's no penalty for a guess, and an unanswered question is a guaranteed zero.
One Last Thing The debrief's first essential was reading comprehension, and your own diagnostic data says the same: nearly half your misses are two things you already know, filed under the wrong label. On exam day the highest-value habit isn't recall — it's reading the stem twice and naming what's being tested before you look at the options.

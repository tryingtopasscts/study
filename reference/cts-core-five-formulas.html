The Core Five

Five formulas. That's what the math on the CTS exam actually is. Learn these cold and roughly one question in six becomes a freebie — no logs, no PAG/NAG, no design-level algebra.

Read This First This page is deliberately narrow. It is not a survey of AV math — it is the five calculations that keep showing up, taught to the point of reflex. If you want the full reference (decibels, loudspeaker impedance, bandwidth, PoE, heat load, cost math), that lives in the AV Math Guide. Come here first. Go there second.

Why only five

Two independent sources agree, and they agree on almost exactly the same list.

Source 1 — AVIXA's own exam blueprint

The CTS Exam Content Outline and Job Task Analysis describe what the 100 questions are built from. Across every domain and task, the outline names exactly two formulas by name:

What the blueprint namesWhere it appears
Ohm's LawDomain D, Task 2 — the only formula called by name anywhere in the outline
Calculating throw distancesDomain D, Tasks 1 and 2 — named twice
Calculating areas and volumesDomain A, Task 1 (site survey)
Calculating sound levelsDomain A, Tasks 6 & 8
Calculating light levelsDomain A, Tasks 6 & 8
Voltage calculationsDomain D, Task 2

Notice what is absent: no PAG/NAG, no jam ratio, no loudspeaker spacing geometry, no conduit capacity formula. Those appear on AVIXA's CTS-D formula sheet — the Design credential, one tier up. If you have been drilling them, you have been studying the wrong exam.

Source 2 — a candidate who just passed

"If I could boil down what you must know to 5 essential topics… Know the basic math and formulas for calculating volume, ohms law, aspect ratio, throw distance, SPL/light change over distance (inverse sq law). … if you have a middle-school level understanding of math, all you need is to memorize a few formulas, and then 10–20% of the questions are freebies." — r/CommercialAV, post-exam debrief

Overlay the two lists and they collapse onto the same five. That's the page you're reading.

1 · Volume & Area

Site survey, acoustics, HVAC, screen area. Pure multiplication — and the most commonly skipped easy point.

2 · Ohm's Law & Power

The only formula AVIXA names outright. Brings impedance and wattage with it.

3 · Aspect Ratio

Diagonal ↔ width ↔ height, plus how far the back row can sit.

4 · Throw Distance

Named twice in the blueprint. One multiplication, three ways to ask it.

5 · Inverse Square Law

Level change over distance — sound and light. One idea, two units.

Honest Caveat "Five formulas" is not a promise that every math question maps to one of them. The exam also touches unit conversion, scale drawings, and percentage/markup arithmetic. Those are covered here as supporting skills (see Conversions), because they show up inside the five rather than as questions of their own.

The 60-Second Brain Dump

Pearson VUE gives you a scratch board. The moment the clock starts, write this out — before you read a single question. It costs one minute and removes all recall pressure for the rest of the exam.

Write these eight lines, in this order
  1. V = I × R — and P = V × I
  2. Zparallel = Z ÷ N — identical speakers only
  3. Volume = L × W × H — Area = L × W
  4. 16:9 → W = D × 0.872, H = D × 0.490
  5. Throw Distance = Throw Ratio × Image Width
  6. dB change = 20 × log(D1 ÷ D2) — sound over distance
  7. Double the distance = −6 dB — light drops to ¼
  8. 1 m = 3.28 ft · 1 in = 25.4 mm
Why This Works Every one of the five formulas is on that board, plus the two conversions that gate them. From that point the exam is no longer a memory test — it's a matching exercise. Read the question, find the line, plug in.
01
Site Survey · Acoustics · HVAC

Volume & Area

The easiest formula on the exam, and the one people lose points on — because the trap is never the multiplication.

In Plain English Area is how much floor (or wall, or screen) you're covering: two dimensions multiplied. Volume is how much air is in the room: three dimensions multiplied. Room volume drives reverberation, HVAC sizing, and loudspeaker coverage decisions, which is why a site survey always captures ceiling height — not just the floor plan.
The Formula
Volume = L × W × H
Area = L × W
  • L — length of the room
  • W — width of the room
  • H — ceiling height (the one people forget to ask for)
Volume is three edges multiplied — nothing more. The exam's difficulty lives entirely in the units those edges arrive in.
The Three Traps 1. Mixed units. A question that gives length in metres and ceiling height in centimetres (or feet and inches) is testing conversion, not multiplication. Convert everything to one unit first, before you touch the calculator.

2. Area when you wanted volume. If the answer choices are in m² you needed two dimensions; m³ means three. The unit on the answer tells you which formula was wanted — read it before you solve.

3. Irregular rooms. An L-shaped room is two rectangles added together, not one average. Split it, solve each, add.
Worked Example
A conference room measures 12 m long by 8 m wide, with a finished ceiling height of 320 cm. What is the room volume in cubic metres, and what is it in cubic feet?
Step 1 — normalise the unitsCeiling height is in centimetres; everything else is in metres. 320 cm ÷ 100 =H = 3.2 m
Step 2 — multiply the three edgesV = 12 × 8 × 3.2 = 307.2 m³
Step 3 — convert to cubic feet1 m³ = 35.315 ft³307.2 × 35.315 ≈ 10,849 ft³
Sanity checkA cubic metre is a big box — about 35 cubic feet — so the ft³ number must be far larger than the m³ number. If your answer got smaller, you divided when you should have multiplied.
Volume & Area Calculator
Floor area uses length × width only. Volume adds the ceiling height.
Why The Exam Cares Room volume is an input to reverberation time, to HVAC load, and to whether a space needs acoustic treatment at all. A site survey question that hands you three dimensions is usually one step in a longer chain — get the volume right and the rest of the question opens up.

Electrical · Troubleshooting · Repair

Ohm's Law & Power

The only formula AVIXA names outright in the exam blueprint. It arrives with two companions you need in the same breath.

In Plain English Voltage is the push. Current is how much actually flows. Resistance (or impedance, in an AC circuit) is what pushes back. Ohm's Law says those three are locked together — fix any two and the third is decided. Power is what the circuit does with that: volts × amps = watts.
The Formula — and its rearrangements
V = I × R
I = V ÷ R R = V ÷ I
P = V × I
P = I² × R P = V² ÷ R
  • V — voltage, in volts
  • I — current, in amperes (not watts — watts is power)
  • R — resistance in ohms (Ω). In an AC circuit the correct term is impedance (Z)
  • P — power, in watts
Cover the quantity you want. If the two remaining letters sit side by side, multiply them. If one sits above the other, divide top by bottom. Cover I in the left triangle and you're left with V over R — so I = V ÷ R.

Impedance: the part that shows up in loudspeaker questions

Ohm's Law questions on the CTS exam frequently arrive dressed as loudspeaker wiring. Two rules cover almost all of them:

Series vs. Parallel
Series: Ztotal = Z1 + Z2 + Z3 …
Parallel (identical speakers): Ztotal = Z ÷ N
  • Series adds up — impedance goes up, the amp works less hard
  • Parallel divides — impedance goes down, the amp works harder. Four 8 Ω speakers in parallel = 2 Ω, which is where amplifiers get destroyed
  • N — number of identical loudspeakers. If they aren't identical you need the reciprocal formula, which is CTS-D territory
The Classic Wrong Answer Amperes measure current. Watts measure power. A question asking for "the unit of current" has amperes as the answer and watts sitting right beside it as bait. Likewise: resistance is the DC term, impedance is the AC term — if the question says "alternating current" and one option says resistance and another says impedance, impedance wins.
Worked Example
An amplifier channel delivers 24 V across a 4 Ω loudspeaker load. How much current flows, and how much power is being delivered?
Step 1 — pick the rearrangementYou have V and R, you want I. Cover I on the triangle: V sits over R.I = V ÷ R
Step 2 — solve for currentI = 24 ÷ 4 = 6 A
Step 3 — solve for powerP = V × I = 24 × 6 = 144 W
Step 4 — verify a different wayP = V² ÷ R = 576 ÷ 4 = 144 W. Two routes, same answer — that's your check.✓ 6 A, 144 W

Enter exactly two values; the third and the power are derived. Clear a field to re-solve for it.

Series wiring instead? Multiply rather than divide: 4 × 8 Ω in series = 32 Ω.

Display · Sightlines · Site Evaluation

Aspect Ratio & Image Size

Screens are sold by diagonal. Rooms are designed by height and width. This is the formula that converts between them.

In Plain English Aspect ratio is just the shape of the rectangle — width compared to height. 16:9 means 16 units across for every 9 up. The catch is that manufacturers quote the diagonal, which is neither of those numbers, so you need a way to get from a "55-inch display" to the actual width and height that determine whether the back row can read it.
The Formula
Aspect Ratio = W : H
For 16:9 — W = D × 0.872  ·  H = D × 0.490
  • D — the quoted diagonal
  • These factors come from Pythagoras: √(16² + 9²) = 18.358, so W = D × 16⁄18.358
  • You do not need to derive them in the exam — memorise 0.872 and 0.490 and move on
A 55" 16:9 display is 48" wide and 27" tall. Multiply that 27" height by 4, 6 or 8 to find how far back the last viewer can sit, depending on what they need to do with the image.

Diagonal factors worth having

The 4 / 6 / 8 Rule The farthest viewer sits no more than 4 × image height for analytical viewing (reading fine detail, spreadsheets, medical), 6 × image height for basic decision-making, and 8 × image height for passive viewing (video, entertainment). The closest viewer sits no nearer than 2 × image height. Read the other way round: image height ≈ farthest viewer distance ÷ 6 for a general-purpose room.
Standards Note AVIXA's current display sizing standard (DISCAS, V202.01) replaced the 4/6/8 rule with a calculation based on the height of the smallest element on screen — %EH for analytical viewing, and a farthest-viewer formula for basic decision making. The CTS Exam Guide 3rd Edition still teaches 4/6/8, and that's what exam questions key off. Know 4/6/8 for the test; know DISCAS exists so the terminology doesn't blindside you.
Worked Example
A client wants a 55" 16:9 display in a small huddle room for general presentation use. What is the image height, and how far back can the last chair go?
Step 1 — diagonal to height16:9 height factor is 0.490H = 55 × 0.490 = 26.95 ≈ 27 in
Step 2 — apply the right multiple"General presentation" = basic decision making = 6 × image height6 × 27 = 162 in
Step 3 — convert to something usable162 ÷ 12 = 13.5 ft (≈ 4.1 m)
Watch the trapIf the answer choices are in feet and you left the answer in inches, 162 will not appear — but 13.5 will. Always finish in the units the options use.

Projection · Troubleshoot · Repair

Throw Distance

Named twice in AVIXA's blueprint — more than any other calculation. It is one multiplication asked three different ways.

In Plain English Throw ratio is a projector's personality: how far back it has to sit to fill a given width. A 2.0:1 lens needs 2 metres of distance for every 1 metre of image. So if you know the ratio and the width you want, the distance is fixed — and if the room dictates the distance, the ratio you must buy is fixed.
The Formula — and its rearrangements
Throw Distance = Throw Ratio × Image Width
Throw Ratio = TD ÷ W Image Width = TD ÷ Throw Ratio
  • Throw Distance — lens to screen surface
  • Throw Ratio — the projector spec, written like 1.8:1 (use the 1.8)
  • Image Width — width, never diagonal, never height
1.8 × 3 m = 5.4 m. The diagram is drawn on its side because the exam often draws it in plan view on a reflected ceiling plan — the dimension you measure is still lens-to-screen.
The Trap That Catches Everyone The formula uses image WIDTH. Questions love to hand you a diagonal or a screen height instead, because that adds a hidden first step. If the question says "a 100-inch 16:9 screen," you must convert to width (100 × 0.872 = 87.2 in) before you multiply by the throw ratio. The wrong answers are built from people who skipped that step — the distractor list will contain the diagonal-times-ratio result, waiting for you.
Worked Example — the two-step version
A projector with a 1.5:1 throw ratio must fill a 120-inch diagonal 16:9 screen. The room allows a maximum lens-to-screen distance of 12 feet. Will it fit?
Step 1 — spot the hidden stepYou were given a diagonal, but the formula needs width. Convert first.W = 120 × 0.872 = 104.6 in
Step 2 — apply the formulaTD = 1.5 × 104.6 = 156.9 in
Step 3 — match the units in the questionThe room limit is in feet.156.9 ÷ 12 = 13.1 ft
Step 4 — answer the actual question13.1 ft needed vs. 12 ft available.✗ No — it does not fit. You need a shorter throw ratio (12 ft ÷ 8.72 ft = 1.38:1 or less).
The trap, for referenceSkipping Step 1 gives 1.5 × 120 = 180 in = 15 ft. That number will be sitting in the answer choices.

Audio · Lighting · Design

Inverse Square Law

Level change over distance — sound and light. One physical idea, asked in two different units, and that's the whole difficulty.

In Plain English Energy from a point source spreads out over a sphere as it travels. Double the distance and the same energy covers four times the area, so any given spot receives a quarter as much. That one fact explains both "SPL drops 6 dB every time you double the distance" and "the light on the screen drops to a quarter when you move the projector twice as far away." Same law. Different units on the answer.
Sound — the answer is in dB
ΔdB = 20 × log(D1 ÷ D2)
  • D1 — the reference (known) distance
  • D2 — the new distance you're asked about
  • Result is negative when you move away, positive when you move closer
Light — the answer is in lux or footcandles
E2 = E1 × (D1 ÷ D2)2
  • E1 — known illuminance at distance D1
  • E2 — illuminance at the new distance D2
  • Same ratio, squared instead of logged — because light questions want a level, not a change
The square in "inverse square" is literal — at 3× the distance the patch is 3 times wider and 3 times taller, so 9 times the area receives the same energy.

The shortcut that beats the calculator

You rarely need the log button. Count doublings:

Distance changeSound (SPL)Light (illuminance)
×2 (double)−6 dB÷ 4
×4−12 dB÷ 16
×8−18 dB÷ 64
×10−20 dB÷ 100
÷2 (halve)+6 dB× 4
The −6 dB Rule Every doubling of distance costs 6 dB. If you remember nothing else from this section, remember that. 1 m → 8 m is three doublings (1→2, 2→4, 4→8), so −18 dB. No logarithm required.
20 log, Not 10 log Distance uses 20 log. So do voltage and sound pressure. 10 log is for power — watts, and acoustic power. Mixing them up is the single most common dB error, and it doubles or halves your answer, which is exactly the size of gap the distractors are built around. Memorise the split: if it's something you can measure with a meter at a point — pressure, voltage, distance — it's 20 log. If it's power in watts, it's 10 log.
Free Field Only The inverse square law describes a point source in a free field — no reflections. Inside a real reverberant room it only holds until you reach critical distance, past which the reverberant field dominates and level stops dropping. Exam questions almost always assume free field; if a question mentions a highly reverberant space, that assumption is what's being tested.
Worked Example — Sound
A loudspeaker produces 95 dB SPL measured at 1 metre. What is the SPL at the back row, 8 metres away?
Step 1 — count the doublings (the fast way)1 → 2 → 4 → 8 is three doublings.3 × −6 dB = −18 dB
Step 2 — apply the change95 − 18 = 77 dB SPL
Step 3 — confirm with the formulaΔdB = 20 × log(1 ÷ 8) = 20 × log(0.125) = 20 × (−0.903)= −18.06 dB → 95 − 18.06 ≈ 77 dB ✓
Direction checkYou moved away, so the level must drop. If your arithmetic produced 113 dB you inverted the ratio — put the reference distance on top.
Worked Example — Light
A light meter reads 800 lux at 2 metres from a fixture. What will it read at 6 metres?
Step 1 — form the distance ratioReference over new:2 ÷ 6 = 0.3333
Step 2 — square it0.3333² = 0.1111 (that's ⅑)
Step 3 — multiply800 × 0.1111 ≈ 89 lux
Sanity check3× the distance means 9× the area, so ⅑ the light. 800 ÷ 9 = 88.9. ✓ Tripling distance is brutal — this is why ambient light control matters more than projector lumens.

The Meta-Skill The Reddit debrief put reading comprehension above the math, and the blueprint agrees — "basic math" appears as a skill, while the knowledge items are all about interpreting drawings and requirements. Before you calculate: underline what you're given, circle what's asked, and check the units on the answer choices. The units alone often reveal which formula was intended.

The Conversions That Gate The Five

These aren't a sixth formula — they're the step hidden inside the other five. Every one of them has cost somebody a question they knew how to solve.

ConversionFactorShows up in
inches → millimetres× 25.4Screen sizing, rack units
feet → metres× 0.3048Throw distance, viewing distance
metres → feet× 3.281Throw distance, room dimensions
m² → ft²× 10.764Screen area, floor area
m³ → ft³× 35.315Room volume
footcandles → lux× 10.764Ambient light, screen illuminance
inches → feet÷ 12Almost every display question
A Free Mnemonic m² → ft² and footcandles → lux use the same factor, 10.764. That is not a coincidence — a footcandle is one lumen per square foot and a lux is one lumen per square metre, so converting between them is the area conversion. Remember one number, get two conversions.
Scale Drawings A drawing marked 1:50 means one unit on paper equals 50 in the real room — so you multiply the measured dimension by 50. Reading the scale backwards (dividing) produces an answer that is off by a factor of 2,500 on an area calculation, which at least tends to be obviously wrong. Watch for NTS (Not To Scale) on a drawing — it means you cannot measure it at all, and the question is testing whether you know that.

What's deliberately NOT on this page

Scoping discipline only works if you know what you scoped out — and why.

Still on the CTS exam, just lower-yield

These appear, but as single questions rather than recurring calculation types. They live in the AV Math Guide:

  • Heat load — BTU = watts × 3.41. One formula, occasionally asked.
  • Projector lumens — screen area × required illuminance. Named in the blueprint under design tasks.
  • Cost math — margin vs. markup, contingency percentages. Domain C is 11% of the exam, but it's arithmetic, not formulas.
  • dB reference levels — dBu, dBV, dBSPL, and the 10 log / 20 log split beyond distance.

CTS-D territory — skip it for this exam

If you find yourself studying any of the following, stop. They're on AVIXA's Design formula sheet, not the CTS blueprint:

  • PAG/NAG and the full audio system stability formula
  • Loudspeaker coverage-circle and spacing geometry
  • Jam ratio and conduit capacity formulas
  • Camera image size from lens angle of view
  • Electrical power required (EPR) at the loudspeaker
The Payoff The candidate quote is worth taking literally: master these five and 10–20% of the exam becomes freebies. On a 100-question test with a scaled pass mark, that is not a marginal gain — it is the difference between hoping and knowing. Then put the recovered study time into cable types, project phase order, and practice questions, which is where the other 80% actually lives.

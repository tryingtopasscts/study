Wireless Radio Systems

Everything in this chapter comes down to four questions: what is a radio wave, how do you get information onto one, what happens to it on the way, and how does an antenna hand it off cleanly at both ends. Short chapter, high density of memorizable numbers.

SECTION 01The 60-second map

Before any detail: a wireless link is always the same five moving parts. Every fact in this chapter attaches to one of them.

Exam tip

This chapter is foundational knowledge — it doesn't map to a CTS duty/task the way Parts III–V do. But its vocabulary and numbers leak into questions about wireless microphones (Ch. 4), Wi-Fi (Ch. 6), RF control (Ch. 8), and rack building (Ch. 17). Learn the numbers cold; they're cheap points.

SECTION 02Radio vs. RF vs. Wireless

Three words people swap freely on job sites. AVIXA treats them as three distinct things, and a question can hinge on the distinction. The trick is that they're not three synonyms and they're not a clean hierarchy — they overlap partially.

Key term

Radio waves — the slice of the electromagnetic spectrum from 3 kHz to 300 GHz, produced by the movement of electric charge. An oscillating current generates an electric field, a magnetic field, and electromagnetic radiation; the oscillation frequency of the current sets the frequency of the radiation.

Radio frequency (RF) — any signal whose frequency falls in that same 3 kHz – 300 GHz range, however it travels. Down a copper coax, over a fiber, on an infrared beam, on a laser — still RF.

Wireless — any signal carried without wire or fiber. Radio waves qualify. So do laser beams and infrared.

Watch out

Two traps live here, and they're mirror images of each other.
Trap 1: "RF always means through the air." No — the coax feeding your TV tuner carries RF signals that are not radio waves.
Trap 2: "Wireless always means radio." No — a handheld IR remote for a projector is wireless communication that uses no radio waves at all.

SECTION 03Where radio sits on the EM spectrum

Radio waves aren't a special kind of thing. They're the low-frequency, long-wavelength end of the same electromagnetic radiation that includes the light you measured back in Chapter 5. Same physics, same straight-line travel, same inverse square falloff — just wavelengths measured in meters rather than nanometers.

Spec

Radio spectrum: 3 kHz (λ = 100 km) to 300 GHz (λ = 1 mm). Both ends, both units. The book's own review question uses "3 kHz to 300 GHz" as the correct answer and offers "9 kHz to 300 GHz" as the distractor — 9 kHz is a real regulatory floor in some jurisdictions, which is exactly what makes it a tempting wrong answer.

SECTION 04Anatomy of a radio wave

One relationship underlies every number in this chapter: frequency and wavelength are inverses. Radio waves all travel at the same speed — the speed of light — so a wave that oscillates faster necessarily fits more cycles into the same distance, which means each cycle is shorter.

What "higher frequency" actually costs you

The book states these as scattered facts. They're all the same fact seen from different angles — shorter waves are more easily stopped by things:

ObstacleLonger wavelength / lower frequencyShorter wavelength / higher frequency
AirPasses easilyPasses easily
Building walls, windowsBetter penetrationPoorer penetration
Metal (conductor)Almost no penetrationAlmost no penetration
Rain, cloud ice crystalsUnaffectedCan be blocked or attenuated
The ionosphereReflects back to earthPasses straight through (below ~100 mm λ)
Key term

Nonconducting materials (brick, timber, glass, plasterboard) attenuate radio waves. Conducting materials — metals — block them almost entirely. This is why an equipment rack, a metal ceiling grid, or foil-backed insulation is an RF problem, not just a cosmetic one.

Exam tip

The ionosphere fact has a practical partner: satellite communication uses very short wavelengths (under 100 mm) precisely because they pass through the ionosphere. If they reflected, you couldn't reach the satellite. Long waves bounce off it — that's how shortwave broadcasts reach beyond the horizon.

SECTION 06Band names and what lives in them

The RF spectrum is chopped into named bands purely for description. You need two of them cold, because they're the two the book names and the two AV work actually lives in — but seeing all eight makes the pattern obvious: each band is one decade of frequency, running 3 to 30 of whatever the unit is.

Spec

VHF (very high frequency): 30 MHz – 300 MHz. Wavelengths 10 m down to 1 m.
UHF (ultrahigh frequency): 300 MHz – 3 GHz. Wavelengths 1 m down to 100 mm.
These two share a boundary at 300 MHz — which, by the 300 rule, is exactly 1 meter. That's not a coincidence, and it makes the boundary easy to recall.

Exam tip

Distractors on band questions are always the adjacent band or the abbreviation that looks similar — VHF for UHF, VLF for VHF. Anchor yourself on the one number: 300 MHz is the line. Below it, VHF. Above it, UHF, all the way to 3 GHz.

SECTION 07Transmitting and receiving

Every radio system needs at least one transmitter and one receiver. The transmitter's job is to take an incoming signal stream — audio from a microphone, control data from a remote, video from a media server, packets from an Ethernet network — and convert it for radio transmission via an antenna.

Modulation: putting information onto a carrier

Information doesn't travel on its own. It gets modulated onto an RF carrier — the book's metaphor is that the carrier is the "envelope" that contains the information. At the far end the receiver demodulates: it strips the carrier back off and hands the original information to the rest of the AV system.

Spec

The carrier frequency must be at least double the frequency of the information it carries. This is the one hard number in the transmit/receive section, and it's easy to skip past on a first read.

Key term

Modulation — the transmitter impressing the information onto the RF carrier. The book calls this the most important step in converting data at the transmitter.

Demodulation — the receiver separating the information back off the carrier so it can be used elsewhere in the AV system.

Watch out

Transmitter modulates. Receiver demodulates. Options that swap these are the standard distractor pair, and under time pressure the two words look nearly identical. Read the stem for which end of the link it's describing before you look at the answers.

SECTION 08Transmission paths

Radio waves are electromagnetic radiation, so they behave like light: they radiate in straight lines from their point of origin. That single sentence generates the whole rest of this section.

Key term

Line of sight — an unobstructed straight path between transmitting and receiving antennas. It's the ideal, not a requirement; reflection can fill in when it's absent, at a cost.

Exam tip

The AVIXA way here: when a question asks where to put an antenna, the answer that wins is the one that establishes direct line of sight and keeps conducting materials out of the path. Not "turn the transmitter power up," not "add an amplifier." Position first.

SECTION 09Allocation of radio frequencies

Spectrum is finite and heavily demanded, so its use is regulated in every jurisdiction and coordinated globally through the International Telecommunication Union (ITU), an agency of the United Nations. The critical practical point: allocations vary depending on where you are.

Watch out

Any question that offers a single worldwide answer for wireless microphone frequencies is offering you a wrong answer. Allocated frequencies and channels vary substantially between countries — and may even vary between cities within one country, because they depend on which bands local terrestrial television broadcasts are using.

Key term

APWPT — the Association of Professional Wireless Production Technologies, an international body that tracks changing spectrum allocations and points to current regulatory sources for event production. It maintains information on at least 30 countries across the Americas, Europe, Asia, and Oceania. Your equipment manufacturer is usually also a good source of local spectrum information.

Exam tip

Chapter 4 pairs with this section: a full-spectrum frequency allocation plan is described there as an important component of any AV installation or production, and it must account for local frequency allocation regulations. If a scenario question involves multiple wireless systems in one space, the AVIXA-correct move is to produce and coordinate that plan — not to hunt for a clear channel on site.

SECTION 10Antennas and orientation

Antennas do the transmitting and receiving. The book is blunt about the stakes: if antennas are incorrectly matched to the equipment, or set up in the wrong position or orientation, overall RF performance degrades. Both failure modes are free to avoid and expensive to diagnose later.

Polarization

Aligning transmit and receive antennas is called polarizing them. Parallel orientation maximizes transmission and reception. A vertically polarized (upright) antenna radiates electromagnetic waves from the sides of the radiating element, in a plane parallel to the floor. Point the receiving antenna the same way and it collects the maximum available radiation.

Key term

Vertically polarized — antenna element upright; radiates from the sides of the element, in a plane parallel to the floor. This is the most common orientation for handheld, body-worn, portable, and installed wireless systems.

Cross-polarized — transmit and receive antennas not oriented in the same direction. The received signal is much weaker and system performance degrades.

Watch out

Cross-polarization is a silent fault. Nothing is broken, nothing reads as an error — the link just has far less margin than it should, and it drops out when someone walks in front of it. On a troubleshooting question, "the receiver antennas were folded down flat while the transmitter is upright" is a complete diagnosis.

SECTION 11Parabolic antennas

A parabolic antenna has a central radiating element feeding into a passive parabolic reflector. The geometry of a parabola does something specific and useful: every ray arriving parallel to the axis reflects to a single point, the focus. That's where the radiating element sits.

Parabolic antennas
Ideal forPoint-to-point communications — high gain toward the focus, high rejection of everything else
Unsuitable whereTransmitters or receivers are in motion, or wide-area coverage is needed
Practical frequency floorabove about 1 GHz — below that the physical dish gets impractically large
Typical AV sightingSatellite television reception
Exam tip

Parabolic questions are almost always testing the same trade-off: directivity is a feature and a limitation at once. If the question mentions movement, roaming presenters, or covering a whole room, parabolic is the wrong answer. If it mentions a fixed link between two known points, it's the right one.

SECTION 12Vertical antennas and the ground plane

Vertical antennas are everywhere in AV for a stack of practical reasons: physically compact, small footprint, easy to mount, omnidirectional in the horizontal plane, and vertically polarized — which matches the most common orientation of handheld, body-worn, portable, and installed wireless gear.

Spec

Quarter-wave element: one of the most efficient configurations for propagating RF from a vertical antenna is a physical length of one quarter of the carrier's wavelength. The book's memory hook: think "Rubber Ducky" antenna.

Ground plane: most vertical antennas are monopoles and need a wide-area connection to ground to radiate efficiently. A quarter-wave vertical needs a ground-plane area about one quarter of a wavelength in diameter.

Watch out

The ground plane relationship is asymmetric, and the exam likes asymmetries.
Bigger than λ/4 → minimal further effect. You gain nothing meaningful by oversizing it.
Smaller than λ/4 → reduced antenna gain and performance. You lose real signal.
So "make the ground plane as large as possible" is not the right answer; "at least a quarter wavelength in diameter" is.

Key term

Ground plane — the conductive surface a monopole antenna radiates against. Easy on a fixed broadcast tower, awkward on anything portable. For many moveable AV systems, the ground plane is the equipment case or the metal mounting rack.

Exam tip

Run the numbers once and the quarter-wave figure stops feeling abstract. A 600 MHz UHF mic has λ = 500 mm, so its element is 125 mm — about the length of the stub on the back of a receiver. A 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi antenna is 31 mm. A 200 MHz VHF system needs 375 mm, which is why VHF antennas are conspicuously long. Use the calculator in Section 5 to check any of these.

SECTION 13Multipath and diversity systems

This section is where the chapter's two halves collide. Omnidirectional antennas radiate in all directions (Section 12). Radio waves reflect off surfaces (Section 8). Put those together in a real room and the receiving antenna gets the same signal more than once, at slightly different times.

Why the signals cancel

The direct wave and the reflected wave travel different distances, so they arrive at different times. Arriving at different times means arriving at different points in their cycle — and if they land roughly opposite, they subtract instead of add.

Key term

Diversity receiver — a receiver using an array of antennas in different places to receive one transmitted signal. It constantly compares the phase differences between the received signals and dynamically selects the best antennas to avoid signal cancellation.

What the receiver does with two good signals

If signals arrive in phase at more than one antenna, the receiver has a choice: it may select the highest-amplitude signal, or it may combine the signals additively. Both behaviours are named in the book, and a question can test either.

Watch out

The classic wrong answer is that a diversity receiver eliminates multipath, or amplifies echoes to strengthen the main signal. It does neither. Multipath is still there; the receiver just has a second vantage point and picks the better one. The correct framing is always compare phase differences → select to avoid cancellation.

Exam tip

Notice the chain of reasoning the chapter builds, because scenario questions walk it in order: omnidirectional radiation → reflections off walls and floors → different arrival times → phase difference → partial cancellation → dropouts. The fix isn't more power. It's a second antenna somewhere else.

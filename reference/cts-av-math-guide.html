AV Math

Every formula on the CTS exam, built for people who haven't touched math in years. Step-by-step worked examples, live calculators, and zero assumptions about what you remember from school.

Exam Tip The CTS exam gives you an on-screen calculator, and the math never goes beyond add, subtract, multiply, divide, square, square root, and the log button. If you can follow a recipe, you can do every calculation in this guide. The skill being tested is picking the right formula and plugging in the right numbers — not mental arithmetic.

Math Foundations

Three skills unlock every AV formula: order of operations, rearranging an equation, and reading a logarithm. Start here.

Order of Operations

When a formula has several operations, you solve them in a fixed priority order. Get this order wrong and you'll get a wrong answer even with the right formula.

  1. Parentheses — anything inside brackets first
  2. Exponents — squares, powers, and roots
  3. Multiplication and Division — left to right, equal priority
  4. Addition and Subtraction — left to right, equal priority
Key Term PEMDAS — the acronym for the order of operations. (You may also see BEMDAS, BIDMAS, or BODMAS — same idea, different regional names.) Multiplication does not beat division, and addition does not beat subtraction; within each pair you simply work left to right.
Worked Example — Order of Operations
A projector spec sheet says: Max throw = (5.05 × SW) + 0.2, and the screen width SW is 2.44 m. Solve it.
Step 1 — Parentheses firstInside the brackets: 5.05 × 2.445.05 × 2.44 = 12.32
Step 2 — Then the additionNow the formula reads 12.32 + 0.212.32 + 0.2 = 12.52
Step 3 — Attach unitsScreen width was in meters, so the answer is in meters.Max throw = 12.52 m

Rearranging a Formula

Most AV formulas come in one arrangement, but the exam may ask you to solve for a different variable. The rule: to move something to the other side of the equals sign, do the opposite operation to both sides. Multiplication moves across as division; addition moves across as subtraction.

Worked Example — Rearranging
You know V = I × R, but you need current (I). Voltage is 2 V and resistance is 8 Ω.
Step 1 — Write the formula and substitute2 = I × 8
Step 2 — Isolate IThe 8 is multiplying I, so divide both sides by 8 to cancel it.2 ÷ 8 = I
Step 3 — Compute and attach unitsI = 0.25 A (amps)
Step 4 — Sanity checkA D-cell battery is 1.5 V and pushes very little current — so a small answer like 0.25 A is reasonable for a 2 V circuit. If you'd gotten 16, you'd know you multiplied instead of divided.
Watch Out Round only your final answer. If you round at every step of a multi-step problem, small errors stack up and your result drifts away from the answer choices. Carry full decimals through and round once at the end (answer keys typically round to the nearest tenth).

Logarithms, Demystified

Every decibel formula uses "log." Here's the whole concept in one sentence.

A base-10 logarithm answers one question: "10 raised to what power gives me this number?" That's it.

  • log(100) = 2, because 10² = 100
  • log(10) = 1, because 10¹ = 10
  • log(1) = 0, because 10⁰ = 1
  • log(0.5) ≈ −0.301 — numbers less than 1 give negative logs

Why does AV use logs at all? Human hearing perceives loudness logarithmically — the range between the quietest sound you can hear and the onset of pain covers a ratio of roughly 3,000,000:1. A log scale squashes that huge range into friendly numbers.

On the exam calculator you just type the ratio and press the log button. But it pays to memorize a few landmark values, because they explain every "rule of thumb" in AV audio:

log(2) ≈ 0.301
log(0.5) ≈ −0.301
log(4) ≈ 0.602
log(10) = 1
log(0.1) = −1
log(1) = 0
Exam Tip log(2) ≈ 0.3 is the single most useful number in AV math. It's why doubling power gives +3 dB (10 × 0.301) and doubling distance or voltage gives ±6 dB (20 × 0.301). If you remember 0.3, you can reconstruct most dB shortcuts from scratch.

The 4-Step Word Problem Method

Exam questions arrive as scenarios, not equations. This routine turns any scenario into arithmetic.

1 · Understand

Read the whole problem. Find your goal — words like calculate, determine, find, and solve for point straight at it. Then list the given numbers and use context clues ("ohms," "volts") to label what each one is. Predict a rough range for a sensible answer.

2 · Plan

Assign variables to your givens and your goal, then pick a formula that connects them. If no single formula fits, chain two together, or sketch a diagram to see the relationships.

3 · Execute

Write the formula down, substitute the numbers, calculate, and attach units. People who skip writing it out make the most mistakes.

4 · Check

Translate the number back into the real world. 95 V in a loudspeaker circuit? Plausible. 50,000 V? You slipped somewhere. A wrong answer reused in later steps causes cascading errors.

Ohm's Law & the Power Formula

Two tiny formulas that generate a whole family of electrical calculations.

Ohm's Law
I = VR
  • I — current, in amps (A)
  • V — voltage, in volts (V)
  • R — resistance, in ohms (Ω)

Rearranged, the same relationship gives you V = I × R and R = V / I. Current is proportional to voltage (more push, more flow) and inversely proportional to resistance (more opposition, less flow).

Power Formula
P = I × V
  • P — power, in watts (W)
  • I — current, in amps (A)
  • V — voltage, in volts (V)

Rearranged: I = P / V and V = P / I. Combining the two laws also gives useful hybrids like P = V² / R and P = I² × R — handy when you know two values but neither formula fits directly.

Key Term Impedance (Z) — the opposition to current flow in an AC circuit, measured in ohms just like resistance. Audio circuits are AC, which is why loudspeaker math uses Z instead of R. The arithmetic works the same way.
Spec The 80% rule: when planning an electrical system, never plan to exceed 80 percent of the rated current capacity of any circuit. A 20 A branch circuit should be loaded to no more than 16 A.
Worked Example — Chaining Both Laws
A device draws 300 W from a 120 V outlet. How much current does it pull, and how many of these devices fit on a 20 A circuit under the 80% rule?
Step 1 — Pick the formulaWe know P and V, we want I. Rearrange P = I × V.I = P / V = 300 / 120 = 2.5 A per device
Step 2 — Apply the 80% rule20 A × 0.80 = 16 A usable
Step 3 — Divide16 / 2.5 = 6.4 → 6 devicesRound down — you can't plug in 0.4 of a device, and rounding up would overload the circuit.

Decibel Math

One concept, three formulas. The only difference is the multiplier out front: 10 for power, 20 for everything else.

A decibel isn't an absolute amount — it's a logarithmic ratio between two quantities of the same kind (two powers, two voltages, two distances). It always answers "how much bigger or smaller is this compared to that reference?" A positive result is a gain; a negative result is a loss.

Decibel — Power
dB = 10 × log(P₁Pr)
  • P₁ — new or measured power
  • Pr — original or reference power
Decibel — Voltage
dB = 20 × log(V₁VR)
  • V₁ — new or measured voltage
  • VR — original or reference voltage
Decibel — Distance (Inverse Square Law)
dB = 20 × log(D₁D₂)
  • D₁ — original / reference distance
  • D₂ — new / measured distance
Watch Out The multiplier is 10 for power and 20 for voltage and distance. Also notice the fractions run in different directions: the power and voltage formulas put the new value on top, while the distance formula puts the original distance on top — that's what makes moving farther away come out negative (a loss). Exam distractors love swapping the 10 and the 20.

The dB Numbers Worth Memorizing

Key Term Inverse square law — sound energy spreads out over a wider area as it travels, so measured at twice the distance, the energy drops to one-quarter of the original. In decibel terms: every doubling of distance from the source loses about 6 dB.
Worked Example — Distance Loss
A listener moves from 1 m to 8 m away from a loudspeaker. What's the SPL change?
Step 1 — Pick the formulaTwo distances → distance formula, multiplier 20, original distance on top.dB = 20 × log(1 / 8)
Step 2 — Divide inside the parentheses first (PEMDAS)1 / 8 = 0.125
Step 3 — Take the loglog(0.125) = −0.903
Step 4 — Multiply20 × (−0.903) = −18 dB (rounded)
Step 5 — Check with the shortcut1 m → 2 m → 4 m → 8 m is three doublings. Three × (−6 dB) = −18 dB. ✓ The shortcut and the formula agree.

Loudspeaker Math

Impedance for wiring speakers together, and the two-step calculation for how much amplifier power you need.

Impedance: Series Circuits

Series Impedance
ZT = Z₁ + Z₂ + Z₃ … + ZN
  • Just add them up. Four 8 Ω speakers in series = 32 Ω total.

Impedance: Parallel Circuits

Parallel — All Speakers the Same Impedance
ZT = Z₁N
  • Z₁ — the impedance of each (identical) loudspeaker
  • N — how many loudspeakers are in the circuit
  • Four 8 Ω speakers in parallel = 8 / 4 = 2 Ω total.
Parallel — Different Impedances
1ZT = 1Z₁ + 1Z₂ + … + 1ZN
  • Add the reciprocals, then flip the result. See the worked example below — it's easier than it looks.
Exam Tip Sanity checks that catch most impedance mistakes: series total is always bigger than the biggest single speaker; parallel total is always smaller than the smallest single speaker. If your parallel answer came out larger, you added when you should have flipped.
Worked Example — Mixed Parallel Impedances
An 8 Ω speaker and a 4 Ω speaker are wired in parallel. What's the total impedance?
Step 1 — Write the reciprocals1/Z_T = 1/8 + 1/4
Step 2 — Convert to decimals and add0.125 + 0.25 = 0.375
Step 3 — Flip it back0.375 is 1/Z_T, so Z_T is 1 ÷ 0.375.Z_T = 2.67 Ω
Step 4 — Check2.67 Ω is smaller than the smallest speaker (4 Ω). ✓ Parallel answers must be.

For series/parallel combination circuits: first use the series formula to total each branch, then treat each branch total as one "speaker" in the parallel formula.

How Much Amplifier Power Do You Need?

This is the classic multi-step CTS problem. You're given a loudspeaker sensitivity rating (e.g., "92 dB SPL, 1 W @ 1 m"), a listener distance, and a target SPL. The plan is always the same two steps:

  1. Distance step: use the inverse square law to find the SPL at the listener with just 1 watt in.
  2. Power step: find how many watts it takes to make up the shortfall, using SPL increase = 10 × log(P₂ / P₁).
Key Term Loudspeaker sensitivity — the SPL a loudspeaker produces from a known power input at a known distance, typically quoted as "XX dB SPL, 1 watt @ 1 meter." It's your reference point for every power calculation. Headroom is extra dB added above the target listening level to handle peaks without distortion.
Worked Example — Power Required at the Loudspeaker
Loudspeaker sensitivity: 92 dB SPL, 1 W @ 1 m. Farthest listener: 8 m. Required level: 70 dB SPL plus 10 dB headroom. How many watts?
Step 1 — SPL at the listener with 1 wattFrom the distance example earlier: going 1 m → 8 m loses 18 dB.92 − 18 = 74 dB SPL at 8 m with 1 W
Step 2 — Find the shortfallTarget = 70 + 10 headroom = 80 dB SPL.80 − 74 = 6 dB short
Step 3 — Set up the power formula6 = 10 × log(x / 1)
Step 4 — Isolate the logDivide both sides by 10.0.6 = log(x)
Step 5 — Undo the log"log(x) = 0.6" means x = 10 to the power 0.6 (the 10^x button on your calculator).x = 3.98 watts ≈ 4 W
Step 6 — Check with shortcuts+3 dB doubles power (1 W → 2 W), another +3 dB doubles again (2 W → 4 W). Six dB = two doublings = 4 W. ✓

Free-field estimate — real rooms reflect energy off boundaries, so actual loss is often less than calculated.

Wavelength & Digital Audio Bandwidth

Two quick formulas from the audio fundamentals chapters.

Wavelength
λ = vf
  • λ — wavelength (m)
  • v — speed of sound: 343 m/s in air at sea level, 20 °C (1,125 ft/s)
  • f — frequency (Hz)

Wavelength and frequency are inversely proportional: low frequencies have long waves (20 Hz → 343/20 = 17.2 m) and high frequencies have short ones (20 kHz → 17.2 mm). That's the full audible span of human hearing, 20 Hz–20 kHz.

Digital Audio Bandwidth
Bandwidth = Sample Rate × Bit Depth × Channels
  • Bandwidth in bits per second · sample rate in Hz (samples/second) · bit depth in bits per sample
Spec The Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem: the sample rate must be greater than twice the highest frequency you want to capture. Human hearing tops out around 20 kHz, so digital audio needs a sample rate above 40 kHz — which is why CD audio uses 44.1 kHz at 16 bits.
Worked Example — Audio Bandwidth
How much bandwidth does stereo CD-quality audio need? (44.1 kHz, 16-bit, 2 channels)
Step 1 — SubstituteBW = 44,100 × 16 × 2
Step 2 — Multiply left to right44,100 × 16 = 705,600 · then × 2 = 1,411,200
Step 3 — Attach sensible units≈ 1.41 Mbps

Aspect Ratio & Screen Sizing

Width, height, diagonal — you'll be given two and asked for the third.

Aspect Ratio
AR = WH
  • W — image width · H — image height, in the same units

Convert a ratio like 16:9 to a decimal by dividing: 16 ÷ 9 = 1.7778. Then two rearrangements do all the work:

Height & Width from a Decimal Aspect Ratio
H = WAR    W = H × AR
Watch Out 16:9 and 4:3 are repeating decimals (1.7777…, 1.3333…). Use at least four decimal places in calculations or rounding errors creep into your answer. And check the display orientation — a portrait display flips the ratio you'd expect.
FormatRatioDecimal
Legacy / standard video4:31.3333
35 mm film3:21.5
HD video / digital TV16:91.7778
Screen Diagonal (Pythagorean Theorem)
A² + B² = C²
  • A — screen height · B — screen width · C — the diagonal
  • To find the diagonal: square the height, square the width, add them, take the square root.
Worked Example — From Width to Diagonal
A 16:9 screen is 2.44 m wide. Find its height and diagonal.
Step 1 — Height from aspect ratioH = W / AR = 2.44 / 1.7778 = 1.3725 mKeep all the decimals for now.
Step 2 — Square both sides of the triangle2.44² = 5.9536 · 1.3725² = 1.8838
Step 3 — Add, then square root5.9536 + 1.8838 = 7.8374 · √7.8374 = 2.7995
Step 4 — Round once, at the endDiagonal ≈ 2.8 m

Sizing for the Viewer: %EH

AVIXA's DISCAS standard (V202.01, Display Image Size for 2D Content) drives image-size decisions from what the viewer must actually see. For basic decision-making (BDM) viewing — presentations, classrooms, boardrooms — calculations are based on percent element height:

Percent Element Height (DISCAS · BDM)
%EH = Element heightTotal image height
  • Expressed as a percentage of the image height. Example: on an HD image 1,080 px high, a symbol with a %EH of 5 is 54 px tall (5% of 1,080). For text, element heights are measured from lowercase characters, excluding descenders.
Key Term Closest viewer / farthest viewer — DISCAS defines viewing distance limits by the nearest position where content can still be comfortably assimilated and the farthest position where the required detail can still be discerned. For analytical decision-making (ADM) — medical imaging, engineering drawings — calculations are based on resolving a single pixel.
Spec BDM placement guidance: top of image no more than 30° above the closest viewer's eye level; viewers within a 60° arc of the far edge of the screen.

Throw Distance & Projector Brightness

Where the projector goes, and how its light output is measured.

Estimating Throw Distance
Distance = Screen Width × Throw Ratio
  • Distance — from the front of the lens to the closest point on the screen
  • Throw ratio — the ratio of throw distance to image width, from the projector's specs
  • Always confirm against the owner's manual — every projector/lens combination publishes its own exact formulas.

Manufacturer spec sheets typically give a max-throw formula, a min-throw formula (the zoom range), and sometimes a vertical offset formula. They look like this sample:

Sample Manufacturer Formulas
Max throw (m) = (5.05 × SW) + 0.2
Min throw (m) = (3.2 × SW) + 0.26
Offset = 0.03 × SW
  • SW — screen width. For a 2.44 m wide screen: max = 12.52 m, min = 8.07 m.
Exam Tip These formulas are pure PEMDAS practice: multiply inside the parentheses first, then add the constant. The constants differ per projector — the exam gives you the numbers; your job is just the order of operations.
Calculator — Throw Distance

ANSI Lumens

Projector brightness is quoted in lumens, measured with the nine-zone method: project a full-white field, divide the screen into nine equal areas, take an incident light reading at the center of each, average the nine readings, then multiply by the screen area.

ANSI Lumen Rating
L = SH × SW × AI
  • L — lumens · SH — screen height · SW — screen width · AI — average illuminance of the nine readings
Watch Out Units must match: if the light readings are in lux, screen area must be in square meters (1 lux = 1 lumen/m²). If readings are in foot-candles, area must be in square feet (1 fc = 1 lumen/ft²). Mixing them is the classic trap.

Heat Load

Every watt an AV system consumes eventually becomes heat the HVAC must remove.

Heat Load
Total Btu = WE × 3.4    Total kJ = WE × 3.6
  • WE — total watts of all equipment in the room
  • 1 watt generates 3.4 Btu of heat per hour, or 3.6 kilojoules per hour
  • Neither formula accounts for the heat load generated by amplifiers.
Worked Example — Rack Heat Load
A rack contains equipment totaling 1,200 W. What heat load, in Btu per hour, must the HVAC system handle?
Step 1 — SubstituteTotal Btu = 1,200 × 3.4
Step 2 — Multiply= 4,080 Btu per hour
Step 3 — CheckBigger than the wattage but same order of magnitude — reasonable. (In kJ it would be 1,200 × 3.6 = 4,320 kJ/h.)

RF Spectrum

Less formula, more "just know this" — but it shows up as straight recall on the exam.

Spec The RF spectrum runs from 3 kHz to 300 GHz. It has a floor as well as a ceiling — a distractor like "300 GHz and below" is missing the lower bound entirely.

Cabling & Rigging Numbers

Installation specs that show up as both straight recall and quick multiplication.

Spec Conduit fill: when running three or more cables through one conduit, their combined cross-section may occupy no more than 40 percent of the conduit's interior area.
Spec Cable bend radius: never bend a cable tighter than 4× its outer diameter — tighter bends risk damaging the conductors or shield inside.
Rigging Safety Factor
Required rating = Load weight × 5
  • The standard safety factor for anything flown overhead in AV/entertainment rigging is 5:1.
Worked Example — Rigging Safety Factor
A 40 kg (88 lb) display and mounting hardware will be flown overhead. What rating does the mount need?
Step 1 — Apply the 5:1 factor40 kg × 5 = 200 kg
Step 2 — Check200 kg (440 lb) is the minimum rating — this isn't a suggestion, it's a life-safety margin covering shock loading and rigging hardware fatigue over time.
Spec RS-485 (used for DMX512): a balanced serial standard supporting up to 32 daisy-chained devices, terminated with 120 Ω resistors at both ends of the run. Don't confuse this with 75 Ω — that's video coax impedance, a different spec entirely.

Site Survey & Drawing Math

Reading a drawing accurately is its own kind of math — mostly proportional scaling.

Scale Conversion
Real-world size = Drawn measurement × Scale factor
  • A 1:50 drawing has a scale factor of 50 — every unit measured on paper equals 50 of that same unit in reality.
Worked Example — Reading a Scaled Drawing
On a 1:50 metric drawing, a corridor measures 20 cm. What's its real-world length?
Step 1 — Multiply by the scale factor20 cm × 50 = 1,000 cm
Step 2 — Convert to a sensible unit1,000 cm = 10 m
Step 3 — CheckA 20 cm mark on paper representing a 10 m corridor is a believable scale relationship. Landing on 1 m instead means the scale factor got divided out rather than multiplied in.

Cost & Budget Math

Percentage math applied to project budgets — the numbers the exam expects you to have a feel for.

Cost Terminology Ladder

Cost figures get more precise — and more binding — as a project moves through design:

1 · Opinion of probable cost

An early, experience-based figure — appropriate for the program report, before detailed design exists.

2 · Estimate

Adds line-item objectivity once more design detail is available.

3 · Quote

Detailed and enforceable — comes after design is finalized.

Spec General contractor markup on an AV subcontract typically runs 5-10% or more. On a $2M system, that's roughly $100,000-$200,000 — a common wrong answer undershoots this by an order of magnitude.
Spec Contingency budgets of 5-10% are most appropriate for projects that are large and long — think $500,000+ and lasting a year or more. Small, short projects don't carry the same exposure to site conflicts, equipment updates, and schedule drift.

01 Gathering Customer Information CH 11 · Duty A ▾

Establish contacts and communication, then run a needs analysis to learn what the users actually need to do — before anyone talks equipment.

Key activities

  • Collect full contact list: architect, building / IT / security / AV managers
  • Conduct initial meetings with client and end users
  • Run a needs analysis — activities → applications → functions
  • Benchmarking: let the client experience comparable systems
  • Obtain scale drawings of the space; identify constraints

Feeds forward

  • Documented needs and functional descriptions
  • Client contact register
  • Building plans & identified constraints
  • Note: this is the input to the program report, not the report itself
Figure · Needs Analysis Pyramid
Tap a section of the diagram to see why it matters.
Validate it both ways: needs flow down to decide everything below; real-world constraints flow up to limit what's feasible. The equipment tier never leads.
Figure · Function-First Chain
Tap a section of the diagram to see why it matters.
The same idea as a pipeline. Outcomes justify tasks, tasks require functions, functions are met by equipment — in that order.
Key term · Needs Analysis Pyramid Needs → Applications → Functions (equipment) → Infrastructure. Top-down or bottom-up, but you never skip straight to an equipment list. The needs decide everything below them.
Watch out Jumping to an equipment list is tempting from a sales angle — but it is not part of needs analysis. Only analyzing needs guarantees the design actually supports them.
02 Conducting a Site Survey CH 12 · Duty A ▾

Go to the site. Observe and document conditions, read the drawings, and measure the spaces you'll build in.

Key activities

  • Observe health & safety requirements; identify the AHJ
  • Gather general site info via the on-site survey checklist
  • Interpret site plans & CAD: views, title blocks, scales, symbols
  • Convert measurements; calculate area & volume of spaces

Feeds forward

  • Completed on-site survey checklist
  • Field-verified dimensions, access & delivery constraints
  • Documented existing site conditions
Spec · Fall protection Falls are the leading cause of worksite fatalities. The AHJ may require approved harnesses and static lines above 6 ft (2 m). Fiberglass ladders preferred near electrical work.
03 Evaluating the Site Environment CH 13 · Duty A ▾

Assess how the physical environment — acoustics, lighting, power, HVAC, structure — will affect (or fight) the AV systems you plan to install.

Key activities

  • Evaluate acoustic conditions & noise sources
  • Assess ambient & controllable lighting vs. displays and cameras
  • Check electrical capacity, grounding, and HVAC
  • Judge structural / mounting suitability of the space

Feeds forward

  • Environmental assessment of each space
  • List of conditions that will constrain the design
  • Basis for recommending remediation (Phase 04)
Exam tip Phases 03 & 04 are a pair: you first evaluate what the environment does to AV, then recommend changes. Keep "assess" and "recommend" distinct in your head — questions test the boundary.
Information-gathering complete
Everything above is discovery. From here the AV team synthesizes findings into recommendations and formal, sign-off-able documents.
04 Recommending Changes to the Environment CH 14 · Duty A ▾

Where the environment can't support the intended AV, propose remediation — and document what other trades must deliver.

Key activities

  • Recommend acoustic treatment, lighting control, power, HVAC fixes
  • Identify work outside the AV contract (electrical to racks, etc.)
  • Coordinate recommendations with architect / MEP

Feeds forward

  • Documented environmental change recommendations
  • Clear scope boundary: AV vs. other contractors
Watch out · Late entry If AV is brought in after the architect has designed the space, some conditions can't be changed — compromising AV performance or adding cost. Early involvement yields better environments.
05 Developing the AV Project Scope CH 15 · Duty A ▾

The program phase. Turn all gathered information into a functional scope and the report that becomes the contractual basis for design.

Key activities

  • Compile customer + site info into a functional AV scope
  • Include end users — they will use and maintain the system
  • Set priorities against budget; account for future needs
  • Estimate equipment + installation cost; note ~5–10% contingency on large projects
  • Present to client; iterate through review & sign-off rounds

Primary deliverable

  • Concept Design / Program Report (a.k.a. "brief")
  • Functions, components, layout, supporting systems, cost
  • May become the basis for an RFP in design-bid-build
  • Distributed to owner, end users, architect, PM, GC, integrator
Figure 15-1 · The Program Phase Process
Tap a section of the diagram to see why it matters.
All discovery converges into one signable document. Approval turns it into the (often contractual) basis for design — and sometimes an RFP.
Key term · Program phase Called the programming phase in the architectural trades. Governed largely by ANSI/INFOCOMM 2M-2010 (Standard Guide for AV Systems Design and Coordination Processes). Once distributed, reviewed, and approved, the report becomes the (often contractual) basis for design.
Exam tip · Objective of the report The program report's job is to communicate overall system capability and budget to decision-makers — not to give a detailed component layout (that's design) or a line-item quote.
06 Designing AV Solutions CH 16 · Duty A ▾

Convert the approved scope into detailed, buildable design documents — the audio, video, control, and display systems specified component by component.

Key activities

  • Design display, audio, control, and signal-management systems
  • Specify components, signal paths, and monitoring / recording needs
  • Produce drawings, schedules, and the bid package

Primary deliverable

  • Detailed design documents — component layout & installation
  • Bid package for integrators (design-bid-build)
  • System spec that defines required performance
Figure 16-15 · Signal-Flow Diagram
Tap a section of the diagram to see why it matters.
Also called a block, flow, or schematic diagram. Convention: sources left, processing middle, outputs right — so signal type, conversion, and routing all read in one direction.
Spec references Design draws on standards such as F502.02 (rack design), display-image-size guidance, and UX701.01 (user-experience design). The performance spec written here is what verification later tests against.
Design approved → build begins
Scope and design are signed off. The project moves out of the office and onto the construction site.
07 Integrating & Installing AV Solutions CH 17 · Duty B ▾

Coordinate with other trades and physically build the system: racks, raceways, cable pulls, terminations, and mounting.

Key activities

  • Hold the kickoff meeting: protocols, RFIs, payment, clean-and-clear dates
  • Attend weekly project status / coordination meetings
  • Build & assemble racks; install raceways, conduit, supports
  • Pull, label, terminate, and test cables (group by signal type; mics separate)
  • Mount components per load / weight and safety requirements

Feeds forward / records

  • Installed, cabled, terminated system
  • Meeting minutes & action items (kept by organizer, distributed)
  • Cable run sheets & labeling nomenclature
  • Updated Gantt-chart project schedule (Ch. 19)
Diagram · Cable Signal Separation
Tap a section of the diagram to see why it matters.
Bundle only one signal type together, and give the weakest signals the most physical distance. Mic level is lowest, so it's the most vulnerable to crosstalk.
Figure 19-3 · Gantt Chart with Dependencies
Tap a section of the diagram to see why it matters.
Bars show sequence and milestones; the dashed link is a dependency — AV electronics can't start until the space is finished, painted, and clean.
Spec · AV comes last The bulk of AV (the electronics) is installed after other trades finish and the site is clean — the gear is sensitive to dust, moisture, and temperature. AV is most involved in the middle-to-final construction schedule.
Key term · Gantt chart The standard way to present the project schedule: horizontal bars for tasks/subtasks, showing sequence, milestones, and dependencies (e.g., walls finished before mounting fragile AV).
08 Verifying System Performance CH 18 · Duty C ▾

Formally confirm the installed system meets spec — the process AV once called commissioning, now systems performance verification.

Key activities

  • Work through a verification list (~150 items in the standard) as a template
  • Calibrate signal paths against test patterns / generators
  • Test audio, video, control, electrical, IT, wireless, documentation
  • Identify & correct issues before client handover

Primary deliverable

  • Project performance verification list & results
  • Test reports (paper + electronic)
  • Objective proof the system meets the contracted spec
Diagram · Verification Categories
Tap a section of the diagram to see why it matters.
The standard's ~150 items span these functional categories. The project's own verification list is built by selecting from them.
Diagram · Commissioning vs. Verification
Tap a section of the diagram to see why it matters.
Construction commissioning runs the whole life of a building. AV's performance verification is one step nested inside it — which is why the terms get conflated on site.
Key term · Verification vs. commissioning In construction, commissioning spans a building's whole life. What AV does is one step within it — so AVIXA calls its role systems performance verification, though other trades still say "commissioning."
Spec · ANSI/InfoComm 10:2013 "Audiovisual Systems Performance Verification" — provides the framework, timing within the delivery cycle, metrics, and reporting procedures across categories from audio to wireless to record documentation.
System verified → ready for handover
Verification confirms the install is complete and ready to operate. Now the system — and the knowledge to run it — transfers to the client.
09 Operating AV Solutions & Handover CH 18 · Duty C ▾

Train the client to run the system and deliver the complete documentation package that closes out the project.

Key activities

  • Train end users & technical staff; "train the trainer"
  • Provide quick-reference / laminated operation guides
  • Document proper startup & shutdown sequence
  • Hand over the full documentation package

Final Documentation Package

  • As-built drawings (paper, DWG, PDF, rack-reduced)
  • All settings/config files: DSP, codec, projector, IP/MAC
  • Test reports; manufacturer manuals; equipment list w/ serials
  • Custom operation guides; troubleshooting guide; spares list
  • Key schedule; warranty statement & service plan
Spec · Shutdown order Sequence matters. For audio, power down amplifiers before mixers so transient peaks don't damage loudspeakers. Provide the sequence as a numbered checklist.
Exam tip Training's goal is user confidence, fewer error-driven service calls, and less misuse damage — not to eliminate the need for maintenance/repair calls. That distractor shows up on the exam.
10 Maintaining Operations & Support CH 20–22 · Duty D ▾

The long tail: preventive maintenance, service records, and troubleshooting/repair that keep the system performing over its life.

Key activities

  • Perform scheduled preventive maintenance at recommended intervals
  • Troubleshoot using signal flow to isolate faults (Ch. 22)
  • Repair / replace; manage consumables (lamps, filters)

Ongoing records

  • Maintenance log: date, technician, problems, corrective steps
  • Service history for long-term serviceability
  • Updated documentation as the system changes
Diagram · The Troubleshooting Trace
Tap a section of the diagram to see why it matters.
Identify symptoms before touching anything, then isolate with one of four strategies. Trace direction differs by subsystem: audio source→output, video output→source.
Key term · Signal flow first Troubleshooting hinges on understanding the signal path — tracing where a signal should be lets a technician pinpoint where it isn't. It's the single most useful diagnostic mental model.
Exam tip · Why log? The main reason to document maintenance is to aid ongoing maintenance and repair — not billing, not warranty, not failure forensics. Keep that framing.

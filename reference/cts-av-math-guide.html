AV Math

Every formula on the CTS exam, built for people who haven't touched math in years. Visual explainers for every concept, step-by-step worked examples, live calculators, and zero assumptions about what you remember from school.

Exam Tip The CTS exam gives you an on-screen calculator, and the math never goes beyond add, subtract, multiply, divide, square, square root, and the log button. If you can follow a recipe, you can do every calculation in this guide. The skill being tested is picking the right formula and plugging in the right numbers — not mental arithmetic.

Math Foundations

Three skills unlock every AV formula: order of operations, rearranging an equation, and reading a logarithm. Start here.

Order of Operations

When a formula has several operations, you solve them in a fixed priority order. Get this order wrong and you'll get a wrong answer even with the right formula.

  1. Parentheses — anything inside brackets first
  2. Exponents — squares, powers, and roots
  3. Multiplication and Division — left to right, equal priority
  4. Addition and Subtraction — left to right, equal priority
Key Term PEMDAS — the acronym for the order of operations. (You may also see BEMDAS, BIDMAS, or BODMAS — same idea, different regional names.) Multiplication does not beat division, and addition does not beat subtraction; within each pair you simply work left to right.
Work any formula in this order, one stage at a time. Everything inside brackets gets fully solved before anything outside them — which is why (5.05 × SW) + 0.2 means "multiply first, then add," never the reverse.
Worked Example — Order of Operations
A projector spec sheet says: Max throw = (5.05 × SW) + 0.2, and the screen width SW is 2.44 m. Solve it.
Step 1 — Parentheses firstInside the brackets: 5.05 × 2.445.05 × 2.44 = 12.32
Step 2 — Then the additionNow the formula reads 12.32 + 0.212.32 + 0.2 = 12.52
Step 3 — Attach unitsScreen width was in meters, so the answer is in meters.Max throw = 12.52 m

Rearranging a Formula

Most AV formulas come in one arrangement, but the exam may ask you to solve for a different variable. The rule: to move something to the other side of the equals sign, do the opposite operation to both sides. Multiplication moves across as division; addition moves across as subtraction.

An equation is a balance scale — it only stays true if both sides get identical treatment. Want I by itself out of V = I × R? The R is multiplying, so divide both pans by R: on the right it cancels away, and you're left with V ÷ R = I.
Worked Example — Rearranging
You know V = I × R, but you need current (I). Voltage is 2 V and resistance is 8 Ω.
Step 1 — Write the formula and substitute2 = I × 8
Step 2 — Isolate IThe 8 is multiplying I, so divide both sides by 8 to cancel it.2 ÷ 8 = I
Step 3 — Compute and attach unitsI = 0.25 A (amps)
Step 4 — Sanity checkA D-cell battery is 1.5 V and pushes very little current — so a small answer like 0.25 A is reasonable for a 2 V circuit. If you'd gotten 16, you'd know you multiplied instead of divided.
Watch Out Round only your final answer. If you round at every step of a multi-step problem, small errors stack up and your result drifts away from the answer choices. Carry full decimals through and round once at the end (answer keys typically round to the nearest tenth).

Logarithms, Demystified

Every decibel formula uses "log." Here's the whole concept in one sentence.

A base-10 logarithm answers one question: "10 raised to what power gives me this number?" That's it.

  • log(100) = 2, because 10² = 100
  • log(10) = 1, because 10¹ = 10
  • log(1) = 0, because 10⁰ = 1
  • log(0.5) ≈ −0.301 — numbers less than 1 give negative logs
In Plain English A log is a counting machine for factors of ten. log(1,000) = 3 because you multiplied 10 by itself three times to get there. log(1) = 0 because you multiplied zero times — nothing changed. log(0.001) = −3 because you divided by 10 three times. Negative doesn't mean a negative quantity; it means "smaller than the reference."

Why does AV use logs at all? Human hearing perceives loudness logarithmically — the range between the quietest sound you can hear and the onset of pain covers a ratio of roughly 3,000,000:1. A log scale squashes that huge range into friendly numbers.

The whole trick in one ruler: the top row is the number, the bottom row is its log. Multiplying by 10 up top just adds 1 down below — so a monster ratio like 1,000,000:1 becomes a tidy "6." Decibels are this idea with a multiplier attached.

On the exam calculator you just type the ratio and press the log button. But it pays to memorize a few landmark values, because they explain every "rule of thumb" in AV audio:

log(2) ≈ 0.301
log(0.5) ≈ −0.301
log(4) ≈ 0.602
log(8) ≈ 0.903
log(0.125) ≈ −0.903
log(10) = 1
log(0.1) = −1
log(1) = 0

Those aren't six unrelated numbers to cram — they're two numbers and a pair of rules. Logs turn multiplication into addition: 4 = 2 × 2, so log(4) = 0.301 + 0.301 = 0.602. 8 = 2 × 2 × 2, so log(8) = three lots of 0.301 = 0.903. And flipping the fraction flips the sign: log(1/x) = −log(x), which is why log(0.5) and log(0.125) are just log(2) and log(8) wearing a minus sign. Exam questions are built so the ratios land on these values.

Watch Out The free answer-checker. Look at the ratio before you calculate. If it's less than 1 — the measured value is smaller than the reference, or the listener is farther away than the reference distance — the dB answer must come out negative (a loss). Greater than 1 → positive → gain. If your sign doesn't match the story the question tells, you've flipped the fraction upside down. That single check catches the most common dB mistake on the exam.
Exam Tip log(2) ≈ 0.3 is the single most useful number in AV math. It's why doubling power gives +3 dB (10 × 0.301) and doubling distance or voltage gives ±6 dB (20 × 0.301). If you remember 0.3, you can reconstruct most dB shortcuts from scratch.

The 4-Step Word Problem Method

Exam questions arrive as scenarios, not equations. This routine turns any scenario into arithmetic.

1 · Understand

Read the whole problem. Find your goal — words like calculate, determine, find, and solve for point straight at it. Then list the given numbers and use context clues ("ohms," "volts") to label what each one is. Predict a rough range for a sensible answer.

2 · Plan

Assign variables to your givens and your goal, then pick a formula that connects them. If no single formula fits, chain two together, or sketch a diagram to see the relationships.

3 · Execute

Write the formula down, substitute the numbers, calculate, and attach units. People who skip writing it out make the most mistakes.

4 · Check

Translate the number back into the real world. 95 V in a loudspeaker circuit? Plausible. 50,000 V? You slipped somewhere. A wrong answer reused in later steps causes cascading errors.

Decoding the Question

Step 2 above says "pick a formula." This section is how you pick it in about five seconds, before you've done any arithmetic at all.

Exam calculation questions are not freely invented. Each one is built backwards from a formula the exam wants to test: the writer picks the formula, decides which variable to hide, and wraps the rest in a scenario. That construction leaves fingerprints. Once you can read them, the formula announces itself.

The One Move: Read It Backwards

Every calculation stem has the same three parts, in the same order:

  1. The scenario — a room, a school, a client. Almost always decoration. It exists to make the question feel real and to hide the numbers in prose.
  2. The givens — every number that arrives with a unit attached.
  3. The ask — nearly always the final sentence, and it names the unit you have to produce.

So read the last sentence first. It tells you what you're solving for. Then sweep back through and collect only the numbers-with-units. The right formula is simply the one that connects what you've got to what they want.

A school gymnasium is being converted into a multipurpose space with a stage area at one end. A cluster loudspeaker system over the front of the stage is mounted 9 m from the floor. The distance from the cluster to the D2 listener is 20 m, and to the D3 listener is 40 m. What is the decibel difference between the D3 position and the D2 position?
scenario — skip it givens — three numbers, all in metres the ask — a difference, in dB

Decoded: metres in, decibels out. That is the distance formula and nothing else. And notice the third fingerprint — the 9 m mounting height is a decoy, a given that no formula needs. Exam writers include spare numbers precisely to see whether you're pattern-matching on "numbers present" instead of reading what's asked.

Exam Tip An unused given is normal, not a sign you've gone wrong. Ask "does this number carry a unit the formula wants?" If the formula needs two distances and you have three lengths, one of them is scenery. Confidently leaving a number on the table is a skill worth practising.

Trigger Words and What They Signal

The prose is mostly decoration, but a handful of phrases are load-bearing. These change what you do:

Watch Out That last row matters more than it looks. A stem can be full of units — "an engineer measures 55 dB SPL A-weighted of ambient noise… how should they proceed?" — and still want a decision, not a number. If the options are sentences rather than values, no formula will help you. Check the options before you start calculating.

Counting the Givens: the Two-Step Tell

Every formula needs a fixed number of inputs. So compare what you were given against what your chosen formula consumes:

  • Givens match the formula exactly → one-step problem. Substitute and go.
  • You're short a value → the missing one must be produced by another formula first. Classic: asked for aspect ratio but given height and diagonal. AR needs width, so Pythagoras runs first.
  • You have more than you need → either a decoy (the 9 m gymnasium ceiling), or a second stage is coming — a target SPL and a headroom figure alongside a sensitivity rating means distance loss then power.
Key Term Chained problem — one where the output of the first formula becomes an input to the second. The exam favours these because a single wrong intermediate value cascades, and the wrong intermediate result is almost always offered as a distractor. Write down each stage; don't try to hold it in your head.

Hidden Steps the Formula Doesn't Mention

A recurring trick: the formula is short, but the scenario adds an operation before you can use it.

  • Out and back. Current has to return. A supply "25 feet away" means 50 feet of conductor for a voltage-drop calculation.
  • Rate × quantity. "0.0062 ohms per foot" is not a resistance yet. Multiply by the length first.
  • Diameter is not radius. Bend radius is 4× the outer diameter; check which one you were handed.
  • Headroom rides on top. "70 dB speech with 10 dB headroom" is an 80 dB target.
  • Match your units to your area. Lux pairs with square metres, foot-candles with square feet. Never cross them.
  • Pick one measurement system. When a stem gives both — "4 m (13 ft)" — work entirely in one and read the answer in the same one.

Ohm's Law & the Power Formula

Two tiny formulas that generate a whole family of electrical calculations.

In Plain English Electricity in a wire behaves like water in a pipe. Voltage is the pressure pushing it along, current is how much is actually flowing, and resistance is how much the path squeezes back. Ohm's law is just the obvious consequence: flow = push ÷ squeeze. More voltage means more current; more resistance means less. Power (watts) is the rate work gets done — push × flow together.
A circuit is a closed loop. The source (left, amber) provides the push, the wire carries the flow, and whatever's in the path — a lamp, a loudspeaker, a device — pushes back (right, red). One push, one loop, one flow: that's why knowing any two of V, I, and R always reveals the third.
Ohm's Law
I = VR
  • I — current, in amps (A)
  • V — voltage, in volts (V)
  • R — resistance, in ohms (Ω)

Rearranged, the same relationship gives you V = I × R and R = V / I. Current is proportional to voltage (more push, more flow) and inversely proportional to resistance (more opposition, less flow).

Power Formula
P = I × V
  • P — power, in watts (W)
  • I — current, in amps (A)
  • V — voltage, in volts (V)

Rearranged: I = P / V and V = P / I. Combining the two laws also gives useful hybrids like P = V² / R and P = I² × R — handy when you know two values but neither formula fits directly.

The cover-up trick: put a thumb over the letter you're solving for, and the layout of the two that remain is the formula. Cover I → V sits over R, so I = V ÷ R. Cover V → I and R sit side by side, so V = I × R. Works identically on the power triangle.
Key Term Impedance (Z) — the opposition to current flow in an AC circuit, measured in ohms just like resistance. Audio circuits are AC, which is why loudspeaker math uses Z instead of R. The arithmetic works the same way.
Spec The 80% rule: when planning an electrical system, never plan to exceed 80 percent of the rated current capacity of any circuit. A 20 A branch circuit should be loaded to no more than 16 A.
Worked Example — Chaining Both Laws
A device draws 300 W from a 120 V outlet. How much current does it pull, and how many of these devices fit on a 20 A circuit under the 80% rule?
Step 1 — Pick the formulaWe know P and V, we want I. Rearrange P = I × V.I = P / V = 300 / 120 = 2.5 A per device
Step 2 — Apply the 80% rule20 A × 0.80 = 16 A usable
Step 3 — Divide16 / 2.5 = 6.4 → 6 devicesRound down — you can't plug in 0.4 of a device, and rounding up would overload the circuit.

Decibel Math

One concept, three formulas. The only difference is the multiplier out front: 10 for power, 20 for everything else.

A decibel isn't an absolute amount — it's a logarithmic ratio between two quantities of the same kind (two powers, two voltages, two distances). It always answers "how much bigger or smaller is this compared to that reference?" A positive result is a gain; a negative result is a loss.

Decibel — Power
dB = 10 × log(P₁Pr)
  • P₁ — new or measured power
  • Pr — original or reference power
Decibel — Voltage
dB = 20 × log(V₁VR)
  • V₁ — new or measured voltage
  • VR — original or reference voltage
Decibel — Distance (Inverse Square Law)
dB = 20 × log(D₁D₂)
  • D₁ — original / reference distance
  • D₂ — new / measured distance
Watch Out The multiplier is 10 for power and 20 for voltage and distance. Also notice the fractions run in different directions: the power and voltage formulas put the new value on top, while the distance formula puts the original distance on top — that's what makes moving farther away come out negative (a loss). Exam distractors love swapping the 10 and the 20.

The One Decision: 10-log or 20-log?

This is where most people lose marks, and it collapses into a single question you ask before touching the calculator:

“Am I comparing watts?”

  • Yes — it's power (watts): use 10 log.
  • No — it's voltage, distance, or sound pressure: use 20 log.
The entire 10-vs-20 decision on one page. Watts take the plain 10. Everything else you'll meet on the exam — volts, metres, pascals — takes 20, because each of those relates to power by a square.

Why 20 and not 10? Because power is the square of voltage: P = V² / R. Double the voltage and you get four times the power. So when you compare two voltages, you're really comparing squared quantities — and squaring a number doubles its log: log(x²) = 2 × log(x). That doubling is what turns the 10 into a 20:

Where the 20 comes from
10 × log(V₁²/V₂²)  =  10 × 2 × log(V₁/V₂)  =  20 × log(V₁/V₂)

Distance gets the 20 for the same structural reason by a different route: radiated energy spreads over an area that grows with the square of the distance — the inverse square law, below. Square relationship → doubled log → 20.

Exam Tip Mnemonic: “Power stands alone.” Power gets the plain 10. Voltage, distance, and pressure all square up into power, so they get double: 20. And note the consequence the exam loves — the same 10× ratio gives +10 dB in watts but +20 dB in volts. Same ratio, different coefficient, different answer.

Key Term Inverse square law — sound energy spreads out over a wider area as it travels, so measured at twice the distance, the energy drops to one-quarter of the original. In decibel terms: every doubling of distance from the source loses about 6 dB.
Sound doesn't get "used up" with distance — it gets spread out. The energy that filled one square at 1 m must cover four squares at 2 m, so each square receives a quarter of it. A quarter of the power is 10 × log(0.25) ≈ −6 dB — which is exactly where the "double the distance, lose 6 dB" rule comes from.

Going Backwards: dB → Ratio

Roughly half of all dB questions run the machine in reverse: "the level rose 6 dB — what happened to the power?" The undo button for a log is 10 raised to that power (the antilog — on the exam calculator it's the 10^x button, usually SHIFT + LOG).

Antilog — Power Ratio
Power ratio = 10(dB ÷ 10)
  • Watts: divide the dB by 10 first, then hit 10^x
Antilog — Voltage / Distance / Pressure Ratio
Ratio = 10(dB ÷ 20)
  • Everything that isn't watts: divide the dB by 20 first, then hit 10^x

It's the forward process mirror-imaged. Forward: divide → log → multiply by the coefficient. Backward: divide by the coefficient → 10^x → that's your ratio.

Watch Out Divide by the coefficient before raising 10 to the power. Computing 10⁶ and then dividing by 10 gives 100,000 — nonsense. The dB has to be shrunk back down to a bare log value first.
Worked Example — dB Back to Watts
A system is 6 dB short of its target SPL, currently driven by 1 W. How many watts does it actually need?
Step 1 — Identify the quantityWe want watts, so this is the power version: divide by 10.Power ratio = 10^(6 ÷ 10)
Step 2 — Do the division first6 ÷ 10 = 0.6
Step 3 — Undo the logPress 10^x with 0.6.10^0.6 = 3.98
Step 4 — Apply the ratio to the current power1 W × 3.98 ≈ 4 W
Step 5 — Check with the shortcut+3 dB doubles the power, so +6 dB is two doublings: 1 → 2 → 4 W. ✓ Also worth absorbing: quadrupling the amplifier bought only 6 dB. Power is expensive; decibels are stubborn.

Reference Levels — "Compared to What?"

A dB is a comparison, so a bare dB figure is meaningless on its own. A dB with a letter after it is simply a dB with the reference baked in. Learn the reference value for each, and whether it belongs to the 10-log (power) family or the 20-log family.

Loudspeaker Math

Impedance for wiring speakers together, and the two-step calculation for how much amplifier power you need.

In Plain English Every loudspeaker pushes back against the amplifier driving it — that pushback is its impedance. The amp never sees individual speakers; it sees one combined total, and that total depends entirely on how the speakers are wired together. Get it too low and the amp overworks and overheats; that's why these formulas matter on real jobs, not just on the exam.

Impedance: Series Circuits

Series Impedance
ZT = Z₁ + Z₂ + Z₃ … + ZN
  • Just add them up. Four 8 Ω speakers in series = 32 Ω total.

Impedance: Parallel Circuits

Parallel — All Speakers the Same Impedance
ZT = Z₁N
  • Z₁ — the impedance of each (identical) loudspeaker
  • N — how many loudspeakers are in the circuit
  • Four 8 Ω speakers in parallel = 8 / 4 = 2 Ω total.
Parallel — Different Impedances
1ZT = 1Z₁ + 1Z₂ + … + 1ZN
  • Add the reciprocals, then flip the result. See the worked example below — it's easier than it looks.
Series chains speakers into a single path — the signal has to push through each one in turn, so the opposition stacks up and impedances add. Parallel gives the signal extra side-by-side routes — more paths means less total opposition, so the combined impedance drops below the smallest speaker. Same two speakers, opposite results: 16 Ω vs 4 Ω.
Exam Tip Sanity checks that catch most impedance mistakes: series total is always bigger than the biggest single speaker; parallel total is always smaller than the smallest single speaker. If your parallel answer came out larger, you added when you should have flipped.
Worked Example — Mixed Parallel Impedances
An 8 Ω speaker and a 4 Ω speaker are wired in parallel. What's the total impedance?
Step 1 — Write the reciprocals1/Z_T = 1/8 + 1/4
Step 2 — Convert to decimals and add0.125 + 0.25 = 0.375
Step 3 — Flip it back0.375 is 1/Z_T, so Z_T is 1 ÷ 0.375.Z_T = 2.67 Ω
Step 4 — Check2.67 Ω is smaller than the smallest speaker (4 Ω). ✓ Parallel answers must be.

For series/parallel combination circuits: first use the series formula to total each branch, then treat each branch total as one "speaker" in the parallel formula.

How Much Amplifier Power Do You Need?

This is the classic multi-step CTS problem. You're given a loudspeaker sensitivity rating (e.g., "92 dB SPL, 1 W @ 1 m"), a listener distance, and a target SPL. The plan is always the same two steps:

  1. Distance step: use the inverse square law to find the SPL at the listener with just 1 watt in.
  2. Power step: find how many watts it takes to make up the shortfall, using SPL increase = 10 × log(P₂ / P₁).
The picture behind every amp-power question: sound leaves the speaker at its rated sensitivity, fades along the way (inverse square law), and arrives short of the target level. Step 1 measures the fade; step 2 buys the shortfall back with watts. That's the whole problem.
Key Term Loudspeaker sensitivity — the SPL a loudspeaker produces from a known power input at a known distance, typically quoted as "XX dB SPL, 1 watt @ 1 meter." It's your reference point for every power calculation. Headroom is extra dB added above the target listening level to handle peaks without distortion.
Worked Example — Power Required at the Loudspeaker
Loudspeaker sensitivity: 92 dB SPL, 1 W @ 1 m. Farthest listener: 8 m. Required level: 70 dB SPL plus 10 dB headroom. How many watts?
Step 1 — SPL at the listener with 1 wattFrom the distance example earlier: going 1 m → 8 m loses 18 dB.92 − 18 = 74 dB SPL at 8 m with 1 W
Step 2 — Find the shortfallTarget = 70 + 10 headroom = 80 dB SPL.80 − 74 = 6 dB short
Step 3 — Set up the power formula6 = 10 × log(x / 1)
Step 4 — Isolate the logDivide both sides by 10.0.6 = log(x)
Step 5 — Undo the log"log(x) = 0.6" means x = 10 to the power 0.6 (the 10^x button on your calculator).x = 3.98 watts ≈ 4 W
Step 6 — Check with shortcuts+3 dB doubles power (1 W → 2 W), another +3 dB doubles again (2 W → 4 W). Six dB = two doublings = 4 W. ✓

Wavelength & Digital Audio Bandwidth

Two quick formulas from the audio fundamentals chapters.

In Plain English Sound is a pressure wave rippling through air — squeeze, release, squeeze, release. Frequency is how many of those cycles happen per second (Hz); wavelength is the physical distance one complete cycle occupies in the air. Since every frequency travels at the same speed (343 m/s), the two are locked together: know one, divide it into the speed of sound, get the other.
Wavelength
λ = vf
  • λ — wavelength (m)
  • v — speed of sound: 343 m/s in air at sea level, 20 °C (1,125 ft/s)
  • f — frequency (Hz)

Wavelength and frequency are inversely proportional: low frequencies have long waves (20 Hz → 343/20 = 17.2 m) and high frequencies have short ones (20 kHz → 17.2 mm). That's the full audible span of human hearing, 20 Hz–20 kHz.

Both waves travel at the same 343 m/s. The lower one simply repeats more often, so each cycle has to fit into less distance. A 100 Hz bass note is a 3.4 m wave — furniture-sized, which is why bass wraps around obstacles; a 10 kHz sizzle is 3.4 cm — easily blocked by a head in the way.
Digital Audio Bandwidth
Bandwidth = Sample Rate × Bit Depth × Channels
  • Bandwidth in bits per second · sample rate in Hz (samples/second) · bit depth in bits per sample
Spec The Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem: the sample rate must be greater than twice the highest frequency you want to capture. Human hearing tops out around 20 kHz, so digital audio needs a sample rate above 40 kHz — which is why CD audio uses 44.1 kHz at 16 bits.
Digital audio is just measurements: the converter checks the wave's height thousands of times per second (the sample rate) and stores each reading as a number (the bit depth). More than two samples per cycle and the wave survives — fewer and it's misread as a lower frequency. That's why Nyquist demands a rate above double the highest frequency, and why bandwidth is simply samples × bits × channels.
Worked Example — Audio Bandwidth
How much bandwidth does stereo CD-quality audio need? (44.1 kHz, 16-bit, 2 channels)
Step 1 — SubstituteBW = 44,100 × 16 × 2
Step 2 — Multiply left to right44,100 × 16 = 705,600 · then × 2 = 1,411,200
Step 3 — Attach sensible units≈ 1.41 Mbps

Stereo CD audio — 44,100 × 16 × 2 = 1,411,200 bps, or 1.4112 Mbps — is the canonical worked example. Even DVD audio at 192 kHz and 24-bit will not stress an enterprise network. Audio bandwidth is almost never the problem; video is.

Video Bandwidth

Horizontal Pixels × Vertical Pixels × Frame Rate × Bit Depth × Subsampling Factor × Transport Overhead

Build it from the smallest component outward:

  1. Number of pixels — horizontal × vertical, the pixels on one screen.
  2. Frame rate — multiply by how many images make up the video.
  3. Bit depth — multiply by how much colour information underlies each pixel.
  4. Chroma subsampling — multiply by the factor for the subsampling level.
  5. TMDS overhead — when transporting over HDMI, add 25%, i.e. factor in 1.25 if applicable.
Chroma SubsamplingFactor to useEffect on colour data
4:4:43No reduction
4:2:22Halves colour data
4:2:01.5Reduces colour data to a quarter
Watch Out

The subsampling factor is not the first number in the ratio. 4:4:4 uses a factor of 3, not 4. Getting this wrong is the single most common error on this calculation.

In Plain English Here is why that factor is 3 and not 4, which makes the whole table stop being arbitrary. Every pixel carries three pieces of information: one brightness value (luma, the "Y") and two colour values (chroma, "Cb" and "Cr"). The name 4:4:4 doesn't count pixels — it describes, for every block of 4 luma samples, how many Cb and how many Cr samples come along with them. So count the samples in a 4-pixel block and divide by 4 to get the per-pixel factor:
NotationPer 4 pixels you get…Total samplesPer pixel
4:4:44 luma + 4 Cb + 4 Cr1212 ÷ 4 = 3
4:2:24 luma + 2 Cb + 2 Cr88 ÷ 4 = 2
4:2:04 luma + 1 Cb + 1 Cr (chroma shared between two lines)66 ÷ 4 = 1.5

The luma count never drops — your eye is far more sensitive to brightness detail than to colour detail, so subsampling throws away colour resolution and keeps every scrap of brightness. That's the entire idea, and the factors fall out of it as simple arithmetic rather than a table to memorise.

Worked Example — Uncompressed 4K Bandwidth
A UHD source runs 3840 × 2160 at 60 frames per second, 8-bit colour, with no chroma subsampling (4:4:4). How much bandwidth does the uncompressed signal need — and will a 10 Gb network carry it?
Step 1 — Pixels in a single frameStart with the picture itself: width × height.3,840 × 2,160 = 8,294,400 pixels
Step 2 — Pixels every secondMultiply by the frame rate, because that whole grid is sent 60 times a second.8,294,400 × 60 = 497,664,000 pixels/second
Step 3 — Bits for each pixelBit depth is per colour component, so 8-bit means 8 bits each.497,664,000 × 8 = 3,981,312,000
Step 4 — Apply the subsampling factor4:4:4 → factor 3, from the table above.3,981,312,000 × 3 = 11,943,936,000 bps ≈ 11.94 Gbps
Step 5 — Answer the question that was askedNo. 11.94 Gbps exceeds a 10 Gb link, and comfortably exceeds the 1 Gb network in almost every building.
Step 6 — What subsampling buys youSwap the factor and rerun only the last step. 4:2:2 uses 2 instead of 3, so two-thirds of the total; 4:2:0 uses 1.5, so half.4:2:2 → 11.94 × ⅔ ≈ 7.96 Gbps · 4:2:0 → 11.94 × ½ ≈ 5.97 Gbps
Step 7 — The takeawayEvery real-world video signal is compressed. Even the most aggressive subsampling leaves you near 6 Gbps. Broadcast and production facilities that genuinely need uncompressed 4:4:4 run 25G networks to do it.

PoE Power Budget

Determine how much power you need, and whether the switch or midspan can deliver it at no more than 80% of its power rating.

Device Power = Voltage × Current
Switch Rating Required = Total Device Power × 1.2
Worked Example — PoE Power Budget
A system uses 4 encoders drawing 48 V at 185 mA each, and 12 decoders drawing 48 V at 140 mA each, all powered over Ethernet. What switch do you specify?
Step 1 — Turn each device into wattsThis is just the power formula, P = V × I. Convert milliamps to amps first: 185 mA = 0.185 A.Encoder: 48 × 0.185 = 8.88 W · Decoder: 48 × 0.140 = 6.72 W
Step 2 — Multiply by how many of each4 encoders: 8.88 × 4 = 35.52 W12 decoders: 6.72 × 12 = 80.64 W
Step 3 — Add for the total load35.52 + 80.64 = 116.16 W across 16 ports
Step 4 — Add headroomNever specify a switch whose rating exactly equals your load. Applying the 120% figure from the formula above:116.16 × 1.2 ≈ 139 W
Step 5 — State the specificationBoth halves matter — the power budget and the port count.A switch supplying ≥ 139 W of PoE across at least 16 PoE-capable ports
Step 6 — Sanity check16 ports at roughly 7-9 W each landing near 116 W is a believable mid-size AV-over-IP deployment. A total in the single watts or the thousands would mean a milliamp-to-amp slip in Step 1 — by far the most common error here.
Watch Out "120% of the load" and "load it to no more than 80%" are not the same number. Adding 20% headroom means × 1.2 (116 W → 139 W). Restricting a switch to 80% of its rating means the rating must be load ÷ 0.8 = × 1.25 (116 W → 145 W). Both framings appear in AV documentation. Read which one the question actually asks for, and use that one.

Aspect Ratio & Screen Sizing

Width, height, diagonal — you'll be given two and asked for the third.

In Plain English An aspect ratio is a shape promise: "16:9" just means every 16 units of width come with 9 units of height, whatever the actual size. Divide the two numbers (16 ÷ 9 = 1.7778) and the promise becomes a single number you can multiply or divide by — which is all screen-sizing math ever is. The diagonal matters because that's how displays are bought and sold, and it comes free from the right triangle every rectangle hides inside it.
Aspect Ratio
AR = WH
  • W — image width · H — image height, in the same units

Convert a ratio like 16:9 to a decimal by dividing: 16 ÷ 9 = 1.7778. Then two rearrangements do all the work:

Height & Width from a Decimal Aspect Ratio
H = WAR    W = H × AR
Watch Out 16:9 and 4:3 are repeating decimals (1.7777…, 1.3333…). Use at least four decimal places in calculations or rounding errors creep into your answer. And check the display orientation — a portrait display flips the ratio you'd expect.
FormatRatioDecimal
Legacy / standard video4:31.3333
35 mm film3:21.5
HD video / digital TV16:91.7778
Screen Diagonal (Pythagorean Theorem)
A² + B² = C²
  • A — screen height · B — screen width · C — the diagonal
  • To find the diagonal: square the height, square the width, add them, take the square root.
One rectangle, three connected numbers. Know W and the ratio → divide for H. Know H → multiply for W. Know both → the dashed diagonal is the hypotenuse of a right triangle, so square, add, square-root. Every screen-sizing question is one of these three moves.
Worked Example — From Width to Diagonal
A 16:9 screen is 2.44 m wide. Find its height and diagonal.
Step 1 — Height from aspect ratioH = W / AR = 2.44 / 1.7778 = 1.3725 mKeep all the decimals for now.
Step 2 — Square both sides of the triangle2.44² = 5.9536 · 1.3725² = 1.8838
Step 3 — Add, then square root5.9536 + 1.8838 = 7.8374 · √7.8374 = 2.7995
Step 4 — Round once, at the endDiagonal ≈ 2.8 m

Sizing for the Viewer: %EH

AVIXA's DISCAS standard (V202.01, Display Image Size for 2D Content) drives image-size decisions from what the viewer must actually see. For basic decision-making (BDM) viewing — presentations, classrooms, boardrooms — calculations are based on percent element height:

Percent Element Height (DISCAS · BDM)
%EH = Element heightTotal image height
  • Expressed as a percentage of the image height. Example: on an HD image 1,080 px high, a symbol with a %EH of 5 is 54 px tall (5% of 1,080). For text, element heights are measured from lowercase characters, excluding descenders.
DISCAS starts from the content, not the screen: find the smallest thing a viewer genuinely has to read — a lowercase letter, a chart symbol — and ask what slice of the image's height it takes up. If the required %EH forces the image bigger, the image gets bigger. Content drives screen size, never the other way around.
Key Term Closest viewer / farthest viewer — DISCAS defines viewing distance limits by the nearest position where content can still be comfortably assimilated and the farthest position where the required detail can still be discerned. For analytical decision-making (ADM) — medical imaging, engineering drawings — calculations are based on resolving a single pixel.
Spec BDM placement guidance: top of image no more than 30° above the closest viewer's eye level; viewers within a 60° arc of the far edge of the screen.

Throw Distance & Projector Brightness

Where the projector goes, and how its light output is measured.

In Plain English A projector's lens spreads light in a fixed-angle cone, so the farther back it sits, the wider the picture gets. Throw ratio captures that geometry in one number: how many screen-widths back the lens must sit. A ratio of 1.5 with a 2 m wide image means the lens belongs 3 m from the screen — that's the entire calculation.
Estimating Throw Distance
Distance = Screen Width × Throw Ratio
  • Distance — from the front of the lens to the closest point on the screen
  • Throw ratio — the ratio of throw distance to image width, from the projector's specs
  • Always confirm against the owner's manual — every projector/lens combination publishes its own exact formulas.
Bird's-eye view. The dashed cone is the lens's fixed spread: back the projector up and the image grows, bring it in and the image shrinks. Zoom lenses give a range of ratios — that's why manufacturer spec sheets publish separate max-throw and min-throw formulas.

Manufacturer spec sheets typically give a max-throw formula, a min-throw formula (the zoom range), and sometimes a vertical offset formula. They look like this sample:

Sample Manufacturer Formulas
Max throw (m) = (5.05 × SW) + 0.2
Min throw (m) = (3.2 × SW) + 0.26
Offset = 0.03 × SW
  • SW — screen width. For a 2.44 m wide screen: max = 12.52 m, min = 8.07 m.
Exam Tip These formulas are pure PEMDAS practice: multiply inside the parentheses first, then add the constant. The constants differ per projector — the exam gives you the numbers; your job is just the order of operations.
Worked Example — Throw Distance from a Spec Sheet
A room needs a 2.5 m wide image. The projector's data sheet gives Max throw (m) = (4.5 × SW) + 0.3 and Min throw (m) = (2.8 × SW) + 0.25. The only available mounting point is 8 m back from the screen. Will this projector work there?
Step 1 — Read what's actually being askedNot "what is the throw distance" — the question is whether 8 m falls inside the range this lens can produce. So you need both ends of the range, not one number.
Step 2 — Max throw, parentheses firstPEMDAS: resolve inside the brackets before adding the constant.4.5 × 2.5 = 11.25, then 11.25 + 0.3 = 11.55 m
Step 3 — Min throw, same order2.8 × 2.5 = 7.0, then 7.0 + 0.25 = 7.25 m
Step 4 — Compare the mounting point to the rangeThis lens can fill a 2.5 m image from anywhere between 7.25 m and 11.55 m.7.25 ≤ 8 ≤ 11.55 ✓
Step 5 — Answer in the terms askedYes. At 8 m the projector sits near the short end of its zoom range, so it will fill the screen with room to adjust. Had the mount been at 6 m, the image would overshoot 2.5 m and you would need a short-throw lens instead.
Step 6 — Sanity checkMin must be smaller than max, and both should be a small multiple of the screen width — a 2.5 m image from roughly 7-12 m is a plausible ratio of about 2.9-4.6. A "max" that came out below the "min" means the two constant sets got swapped.

ANSI Lumens

Projector brightness is quoted in lumens, measured with the nine-zone method: project a full-white field, divide the screen into nine equal areas, take an incident light reading at the center of each, average the nine readings, then multiply by the screen area.

Why nine readings instead of one? Projectors are brightest dead-center and dimmer toward the corners. Averaging a reading from the middle of nine equal zones keeps the rating honest — then multiplying by screen area converts light-per-square-meter (lux) into total light output (lumens).
ANSI Lumen Rating
L = SH × SW × AI
  • L — lumens · SH — screen height · SW — screen width · AI — average illuminance of the nine readings
Watch Out Units must match: if the light readings are in lux, screen area must be in square meters (1 lux = 1 lumen/m²). If readings are in foot-candles, area must be in square feet (1 fc = 1 lumen/ft²). Mixing them is the classic trap.
Worked Example — ANSI Lumens from Nine Readings
A 16:9 screen is 2.44 m wide. Nine incident readings across a full-white field give: 520, 495, 480, 540, 610, 530, 470, 455, and 480 lux. What is the projector's ANSI lumen output?
Step 1 — Notice what you were not givenThe formula needs screen height, and you only have width. But you were told 16:9, which is the missing link. This is a chained problem.
Step 2 — Get the height from the aspect ratioHeight = width ÷ aspect ratio.2.44 ÷ 1.7778 = 1.372 m
Step 3 — Average the nine readingsAdd them all, divide by nine. This is the "AI" in the formula — never use the brightest center reading on its own.520+495+480+540+610+530+470+455+480 = 4,5804,580 ÷ 9 = 508.9 lux
Step 4 — Check your units line upThe readings are in lux, so the area must be in square metres. Both are metric here, so no conversion is needed. Had the readings been foot-candles, the screen would have to be measured in feet.
Step 5 — Apply the formulaL = SH × SW × AI = 1.372 × 2.44 × 508.91.372 × 2.44 = 3.348 m² of screen area3.348 × 508.9 ≈ 1,704 lumens
Step 6 — Sanity checkAround 1,700 lumens is believable for a small meeting-room projector. Note how far the answer sits from the 610 lux hot spot at centre — that gap is exactly why ANSI insists on nine readings rather than one.

Heat Load

Every watt an AV system consumes eventually becomes heat the HVAC must remove.

In Plain English An equipment rack is, thermodynamically, a space heater with features. Once the light has left the lens and the sound has left the speakers, essentially every watt the gear consumed has turned into heat in the room — and the air conditioning has to carry it back out. The formula just converts electrical units (watts) into the units HVAC engineers size systems in (Btu/h or kJ/h).
Watts in, heat out — there's nowhere else for the energy to go. Multiply equipment watts by 3.4 for Btu per hour (3.6 for kJ/h) and hand that number to the mechanical engineer. Remember the stated exception: these formulas don't account for amplifier heat.
Heat Load
Total Btu = WE × 3.4    Total kJ = WE × 3.6
  • WE — total watts of all equipment in the room
  • 1 watt generates 3.4 Btu of heat per hour, or 3.6 kilojoules per hour
  • Neither formula accounts for the heat load generated by amplifiers.
Worked Example — Rack Heat Load
A rack contains equipment totaling 1,200 W. What heat load, in Btu per hour, must the HVAC system handle?
Step 1 — SubstituteTotal Btu = 1,200 × 3.4
Step 2 — Multiply= 4,080 Btu per hour
Step 3 — CheckBigger than the wattage but same order of magnitude — reasonable. (In kJ it would be 1,200 × 3.6 = 4,320 kJ/h.)

RF Spectrum

Less formula, more "just know this" — but it shows up as straight recall on the exam.

Spec The RF spectrum runs from 3 kHz to 300 GHz. It has a floor as well as a ceiling — a distractor like "300 GHz and below" is missing the lower bound entirely.
Nine factors of ten, drawn on a log scale — everything wireless lives somewhere on this band. For the exam, the two amber endpoints are the answer: 3 kHz to 300 GHz, floor and ceiling.

Cabling & Rigging Numbers

Installation specs that show up as both straight recall and quick multiplication.

Spec Conduit fill: when running three or more cables through one conduit, their combined cross-section may occupy no more than 40 percent of the conduit's interior area.
End-on view of a conduit. The 40% limit isn't bureaucracy: cables need free space to shed heat, and you physically have to pull them through without wrecking their jackets. Cross-section area, not cable count, is what's limited.
Spec Cable bend radius: never bend a cable tighter than 4× its outer diameter — tighter bends risk damaging the conductors or shield inside.
Inside the jacket are conductors, foil, and braid that only tolerate so much curving before they kink or tear. Measure the cable's outer diameter D, multiply by four — the bend's radius R may never be tighter. An 8 mm cable turns no tighter than a 32 mm radius.
Rigging Safety Factor
Required rating = Load weight × 5
  • The standard safety factor for anything flown overhead in AV/entertainment rigging is 5:1.
Overhead means over people. The 5:1 factor covers what the arithmetic can't see — shock loads when something snags, hardware fatigue over years, imperfect installation — and it applies to every component in the chain: the mount, the hardware, and the structure it bolts to.
Worked Example — Rigging Safety Factor
A 40 kg (88 lb) display and mounting hardware will be flown overhead. What rating does the mount need?
Step 1 — Apply the 5:1 factor40 kg × 5 = 200 kg
Step 2 — Check200 kg (440 lb) is the minimum rating — this isn't a suggestion, it's a life-safety margin covering shock loading and rigging hardware fatigue over time.
Spec RS-485 (used for DMX512): a balanced serial standard supporting up to 32 daisy-chained devices, terminated with 120 Ω resistors at both ends of the run. Don't confuse this with 75 Ω — that's video coax impedance, a different spec entirely.

Site Survey & Drawing Math

Reading a drawing accurately is its own kind of math — mostly proportional scaling.

Scale Conversion
Real-world size = Drawn measurement × Scale factor
  • A 1:50 drawing has a scale factor of 50 — every unit measured on paper equals 50 of that same unit in reality.
A scale drawing shrinks the building by one fixed factor, so reading it is always the same two moves: measure on paper, multiply by the scale factor — then convert to a sensible unit. If your answer feels like dollhouse furniture, you divided when you should have multiplied.
Worked Example — Reading a Scaled Drawing
On a 1:50 metric drawing, a corridor measures 20 cm. What's its real-world length?
Step 1 — Multiply by the scale factor20 cm × 50 = 1,000 cm
Step 2 — Convert to a sensible unit1,000 cm = 10 m
Step 3 — CheckA 20 cm mark on paper representing a 10 m corridor is a believable scale relationship. Landing on 1 m instead means the scale factor got divided out rather than multiplied in.

Cost & Budget Math

Percentage math applied to project budgets — the numbers the exam expects you to have a feel for.

Cost Terminology Ladder

Cost figures get more precise — and more binding — as a project moves through design:

1 · Opinion of probable cost

An early, experience-based figure — appropriate for the program report, before detailed design exists.

2 · Estimate

Adds line-item objectivity once more design detail is available.

3 · Quote

Detailed and enforceable — comes after design is finalized.

Same project, three stages of certainty. Each bar is the range the real price could land in: an early opinion of probable cost is honest but wide, line-item estimating narrows it, and a quote is a commitment you can hold someone to. The exam tests whether you match the term to the design stage it belongs to.
Spec General contractor markup on an AV subcontract typically runs 5-10% or more. On a $2M system, that's roughly $100,000-$200,000 — a common wrong answer undershoots this by an order of magnitude.
Spec Contingency budgets of 5-10% are most appropriate for projects that are large and long — think $500,000+ and lasting a year or more. Small, short projects don't carry the same exposure to site conflicts, equipment updates, and schedule drift.

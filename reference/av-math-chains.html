Finding the Path

Knowing every formula still leaves one problem: working out which ones, in what order. This is the procedure for that — how to find a route you have never seen before, the five signals that a second step is coming, and the dozen chains that actually turn up.

OneWork backwards, never forwards

The instinct is to start from the numbers you were given and see what you can make. That is the wrong direction, and it is why multi-step questions feel impossible. Forwards, your givens combine into dozens of things you could compute, almost all of them useless. There is no signal telling you which branch leads anywhere.

Backwards, the search is tiny. Start at the ask. Usually only one or two formulas in the whole of AV math produce the quantity you were asked for. Pick one, look at what it needs, and check that against your givens. Anything you are missing becomes a smaller question of exactly the same shape — and you repeat.

the whole method What produces the ask? What does that need? Do I have it? If yes, you are done. If no, the missing input is your new ask — go round again. Two laps solves essentially every chained question on the exam.
The loop is the entire strategy. Each lap either finishes the problem or replaces your question with a strictly easier one, so it cannot wander — unlike working forwards, which has nothing to aim at. In practice the exam never needs more than two laps, which is why a chained question is only ever two formulas deep.
Name the ask, and its unit

Read the last sentence first. Write the unit down — ohms, watts, dB SPL, metres. The unit is what you are hunting for, and half the wrong answers are the right number in the wrong unit.

List the givens, with units

Sweep back through the stem and collect only numbers that arrive with a unit attached. Room names and client details are scenery.

Ask what produces the ask

Not "what can I do with these numbers" — what formula ends in the thing I want. Section 2 is the lookup for this, and it is deliberately organised by output rather than by input.

Check that formula's inputs against your givens

All present → one step, substitute and go. One missing → that missing value is now your ask, and you go back to step 3 for it. Something left over → a decoy, or the bridge you have not spotted yet.

Run the chain forwards, labelling every intermediate

Only now do you calculate, in the reverse of the order you searched. Write each intermediate on its own line with its unit attached.

ThreeFive tells that a second step is coming

You often do not need to run the search at all — the stem announces itself. These are the five signals, in rough order of how often they show up.

1 · You are one input short

You know the formula, you just cannot fill it. Asked for aspect ratio but handed height and diagonal: AR needs width, so something has to produce width first. This is the plain vanilla chain, and it is the most common of the five.

2 · A given is left over

A number with a unit that your chosen formula does not consume. It is one of two things: a decoy, or the bridge into step two. Tell them apart by asking "is there a formula that takes this and produces something I need?" The 9 m ceiling in the gymnasium question produces nothing anyone wants — decoy. A 10 dB headroom figure sitting next to a target SPL feeds straight into the target — bridge.

3 · The units do not meet

Lay your givens' units beside the ask's unit and look for a single formula spanning them. Watts + volts → ohms has no such formula. Amps → Btu has no such formula. When nothing spans the gap, a chain is not optional, it is forced.

4 · A phrase that implies a setup operation

Some wording adds an operation before the formula can be used at all. @ 1 W / 1 m or "sensitivity" anchors a loudspeaker problem and always means two steps. "Ohms per foot" is a rate, not a resistance. "25 feet away" means 50 feet of conductor. "Plus 10 dB of headroom" changes the target before you compare to it. "Within tolerance" means calculate, then compare.

5 · Change versus level

The most missed of the five. 20 log(D₁/D₂) returns a change in dB. If the ask is a level — "what is the SPL at that seat" — the formula's output is an intermediate, not the answer, and you still have to apply it to the SPL you started from. The bare change is always offered as an option, and it is always wrong for a level question.

the sanity check that catches all five Before writing an answer down, put its unit beside the ask's unit. A dB where the question wanted dB SPL, ohms where it wanted amps, a bare ratio where it wanted watts — every one of those is a step short, not a slip in arithmetic.

FourThe chain catalogue

These are the routes that actually appear. Learn to recognise them on sight and most multi-step questions stop being a search at all — you spot the shape and run it.

Electrical

Watts and volts → how many fit on a circuit
Triggered by: a device rated in watts, a supply voltage, and "how many" or "maximum planned".
P, V→I = P / V→rating × 0.8→divide, round DOWN
Wrong turn: rounding up. You cannot install 0.4 of a device, and rounding up overloads the circuit. This is the one chain where the final step rounds down.
A device in amps joining a total in watts
Triggered by: one load quoted in amps while everything else is in watts, then a heat or capacity question.
A, V→P = I × V→add to the total→× 3.4 for Btu
Wrong turn: applying the heat constant to the rack total and forgetting the new device, because it never looked like watts. Units that do not match across a list are always the first thing to reconcile.
Watts and volts → ohms
Triggered by: power and voltage given, resistance or impedance asked for. No single formula spans it.
P, V→I = P / V→R = V / I
Wrong turn: inventing R = P / V, which does not exist. If you cannot name the formula, you have not found the path — you have guessed one.
Voltage drop, end to end
Triggered by: ohms per foot, a distance, a current, and usually a tolerance percentage.
r, D, I→2 × D→× r = Rtotal→V = I × R→vs Vsupply × tol%
Wrong turn: two of them, and both are offered. Skipping the doubling gives exactly half. Taking the tolerance percentage of the drop instead of the supply gives a meaningless number that still looks like arithmetic.

Decibels and loudspeakers

Distance change → a new SPL
Triggered by: a known SPL at one distance, and the ask naming a level at another.
SPL₁, D₁, D₂→20 log(D₁ / D₂)→apply to SPL₁
Wrong turn: answering with the change. −15.6 dB is a correct intermediate and a wrong answer; the question asked for a level, so the last step is not optional. This is tell #5 in the flesh.
Sensitivity → distance → watts
Triggered by: "XX dB SPL, 1 W @ 1 m" plus a listener distance plus a target, often with headroom. The signature multi-step question of the whole exam.
sens, D, target→20 log(1 / D)→+ sensitivity = SPL at 1 W→target + headroom→shortfall→10^(shortfall / 10)
Wrong turn: dropping the headroom. Leaving it out compares 74 dB against a 70 dB target and concludes you already have enough power — a wrong answer that feels satisfying because it comes out clean.
Mixed series and parallel wiring
Triggered by: a described wiring topology with both words in it.
the topology→collapse the innermost group→combine outward
Wrong turn: adding everything, or reciprocal-ing everything. Solve it like brackets in arithmetic — innermost first, treating each collapsed group as a single speaker.
Parallel total → is the amplifier safe?
Triggered by: a speaker count, a parallel wiring, and an amplifier minimum impedance.
Z, N→Z₁ / N→compare vs the amp minimum
Wrong turn: stopping at the impedance. The ask was whether it is safe, so the comparison is the answer — the number is the intermediate.
Mixed parallel impedance
Triggered by: different impedances in parallel. The shortcut does not apply.
Z₁, Z₂ …→add the reciprocals→flip the sum back over
Wrong turn: leaving the reciprocal as the answer. Check it against the rule: a parallel total is always smaller than the smallest speaker. 0.375 Ω from an 8 and a 4 fails that instantly.

Video and install

Height and diagonal → aspect ratio
Triggered by: any two of width / height / diagonal, with the ask needing the pair you do not have.
H, D→A² + B² = C²→AR = W / H
Wrong turn: dividing the two numbers you were handed. Height over diagonal is not an aspect ratio, but it produces a plausible-looking decimal.
Width and aspect ratio → ANSI lumens
Triggered by: an illuminance average and a screen width only, with the aspect ratio mentioned as if in passing.
W, AR, AI→H = W ÷ AR→L = SH × SW × AI
Wrong turn: missing that you were short a dimension at all. An aspect ratio you seem not to need is one of the most reliable signs of a chain.
Per-device power → switch specification
Triggered by: PoE devices quoted in volts and milliamps, several counts of them, and a switch to specify.
V, mA, counts→mA → A→P = V × I each→× count, sum→× 1.2, and count the ports
Wrong turn: leaving the current in milliamps, which lands you a thousand times off. And answering with watts alone when the ask wanted a switch — the port count is half the specification.

FiveWatching the search run

The procedure is easier to trust once you have seen it grind through a problem you could not see the route to. Here it is in full, twice.

"You are given a device's power draw and the supply voltage, and asked what resistance it presents."
askresistance, in ohms
havewatts, volts
lap 1what produces ohms?  R = V / I
needsvolts ✓ — I have that
needsamps ✗ — I do not
new askamps
lap 2what produces amps?  I = V / R needs ohms, which is what I am trying to find — dead end.  I = P / V?
needswatts ✓   volts ✓
pathI = P / V  →  R = V / I

Notice lap 2 rejecting I = V / R because it needs the very thing you are solving for. That is a circular branch, and spotting it is why the search terminates instead of going round forever. If every route into a quantity needs something downstream of it, you picked the wrong producer one lap earlier.

"A 16:9 screen is 2.44 m wide. Nine readings average 509 lux. What is the projector's ANSI lumen output?"
asklumens
have2.44 m of width, 509 lux, and the words "16:9"
lap 1what produces lumens?  L = SH × SW × AI
needsscreen width ✓   average illuminance ✓
needsscreen height ✗
new askscreen height
lap 2what produces height?  H = W ÷ AR
needswidth ✓   aspect ratio ✓ — that is what "16:9" was for
pathH = W ÷ 1.7778  →  L = SH × SW × AI

The aspect ratio was tell #2 — a given left over, which turned out to be the bridge rather than a decoy. You could not have known that reading forwards. Reading backwards, the search asked for a height and the only thing capable of producing one was sitting right there.

SixRunning a chain without losing it

Finding the path is most of the work, but chained questions punish sloppy execution harder than single-step ones, because a wrong intermediate cascades and the wrong intermediate is almost always an offered answer.

Write each intermediate on its own line, with its unit

50 ft → 0.31 Ω → 0.558 V. Three short lines beat one long expression held in your head, and if you slip you can see which stage did it rather than starting over.

Never round an intermediate

Carry full precision and round once, at the end. Rounding at each stage compounds, and the compounded value is frequently one of the options — placed there deliberately.

Sanity-check every intermediate before using it

A parallel impedance larger than the smallest speaker, a screen height larger than its width, an amplifier needing 4,000 W for a meeting room: catch it at the stage it happened. Feeding a nonsense intermediate into step two wastes the step and produces a confident wrong answer.

the trap this whole section exists for Your intermediate result is on the answer list. Every chained question offers it. Seeing your number among the options is not confirmation you are finished — check it against the ask's unit first. A dB where dB SPL was wanted, an impedance where a yes/no was wanted, watts where a switch spec was wanted: all correct arithmetic, all marked wrong.

SevenWhen there is no path at all

Some stems are stuffed with units and want no formula whatsoever. "An engineer measures 55 dB SPL A-weighted of ambient noise when the room is empty. How should they proceed?" — every number is real, and none of them is the point.

The giveaway is never in the stem. It is in the options: sentences instead of values. So make it a habit to glance at the answer list before starting the search. It costs two seconds and it is the only defence against solving a problem nobody asked. The same glance catches the reverse case too — options that are all formulas tell you a calculation really is wanted, and options that pair a value with a verdict ("0.558 volts, and it is in tolerance") tell you the answer has two halves and both must be right.

in one line Read the ask. Read the options. Then search backwards from the ask, and stop only when your answer's unit matches the ask's unit.

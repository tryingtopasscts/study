PART II · CHAPTER 3

Analog and Digital Signals

The foundation chapter: what makes a signal analog or digital, how analog becomes digital, and why digital won.

KEY TERM Analog = continuous variation, directly analogous to a physical quantity — the book's image is a dimmer knob with infinite positions. Digital = discrete states only — a ceiling fan with speeds 1/2/3 and nothing between. Digital waveforms swing rapidly between high (1) and low (0); analog flows continuously.

The A-to-D Pipeline

Sampling

Taking a snapshot of the analog level at regular intervals. The sampling rate (Hz = samples/second) sets how accurately the signal's frequency content is captured.

Digitization & bit depth

Each sample gets converted to a binary value. Bit depth sets amplitude precision: 1 bit = 2 states, 2 bits = 4, 3 bits = 8 — doubling with every bit. 16-bit = 65,536 levels.

Quantization error

The rounding gap between the true analog level and the nearest available digital level. More bits = finer levels = less error. This is why bit depth matters.

Nyquist–Shannon

Reconstruction is possible only if you sample at more than 2× the highest frequency present. 20 kHz audio → >40 kHz sampling → CD's 44.1 kHz. Blu-ray audio: 48/96/192 kHz.

A-to-D in two moves: sampling rate captures frequency; bit depth captures amplitude. Nyquist governs move 1; quantization error haunts move 2.
\n

Compression & Noise

Lossless

Reconstructs the original perfectly (ZIP, FLAC, PNG). Required for text, financial data, program code — anywhere a changed bit changes meaning.

Lossy

Permanently discards data humans are unlikely to perceive (MP3, JPEG, H.264/HEVC). Particularly suitable for audio, video, and images — big savings, acceptable loss.

Codec

A device or processing system that encodes and decodes data. The word is the definition — it comes up as a straight vocab question.

Why digital wins on noise

Digital circuitry decides whether an incoming signal is meant to be high or low, then retransmits it clean — noise is stripped at every regeneration. Analog re-amplification amplifies the noise too, and after many generations noise overcomes the signal.

Ch. 3 — Most Tested

  • Analog = continuous; digital = discrete on/off states
  • Sampling rate → frequency accuracy; bit depth → amplitude precision; each added bit doubles the states
  • Nyquist: sample > 2× highest frequency (44.1 kHz for 20 kHz audio)
  • Lossy is suited to AV content; lossless for data that must survive exactly
  • Codec = encodes and decodes; digital regenerates cleanly, analog accumulates noise
PART II · CHAPTER 4

Audio Systems

Sound physics, then the full electrical chain: mic → mixer → processor → amplifier → loudspeaker. The densest chapter in Part II.

Sound Physics

FACTVALUEWHAT IT MEANS
What sound isCompression waves in a compressible mediumAlternating compression and rarefaction radiating from a vibration. A vacuum isn't compressible — no medium, no sound.
Speed of sound343 m/s (1,125 ft/s) — air, 20°C, sea levelSets every wavelength calculation. λ = v ÷ f: a 1 kHz tone is ~0.34 m long; a 20 Hz bass wave is ~17 m.
Frequency ↔ wavelengthInversely relatedFrequency = cycles per second (Hz); wavelength = distance covering one cycle. Higher pitch = shorter wave.
Just-noticeable difference±3 dBThe smallest change in SPL most listeners can detect. Also happens to equal a doubling/halving of power — two facts, one number.
Fourier analysisAny sound = sum of simple sine wavesEvery complex waveform decomposes into component sine frequencies. This math is the working heart of all DSP.
ReverberationNumerous, persistent reflectionsDistinct from a discrete echo. Ambient noise = any sound that interferes with the desired signal.

Microphones

Polar patterns — the shape is the pickup sensitivity by direction. Dashed circle = omni reference. Match the pattern to what you want to reject.

Transduction

Converting energy between forms. A mic converts acoustic → electrical; a loudspeaker converts electrical → acoustic. Transducers bookend the audio path.

Dynamic mic

Coil on a diaphragm moving in a magnetic field induces voltage. Economical, durable, handles high SPL, needs no power. Output is a tiny mic-level signal.

Condenser mic

A capacitor: charged diaphragm + backplate with air as the dielectric. Requires power to polarize — hence phantom power. More sensitive and detailed than dynamics.

Phantom power

Remote DC power, 12–48 V (48 V most common), applied equally to both signal conductors of a balanced circuit, returning via the shield. Because it's symmetric, it doesn't disturb the audio and can't damage dynamic mics.

Physical formats

Handheld (shock-mounted) · boundary/surface (ceiling mounting = worst performance — far from talkers, near HVAC and projector noise) · gooseneck (lecterns) · shotgun (narrow pattern, film/TV) · lavalier & headmic (usually electret) · beamforming array (DSP-steered pickup beams).

Wireless mics

RF link replacing the cable. Multiple simultaneous systems require frequency coordination. Even the best wireless link is less reliable than a cable. Diversity receivers fight dropouts (see Ch. 10).

Mic cabling

Shielded twisted-pair terminated in 3-pin XLR — female at the mic end, male toward the mixer. Twisting provides common-mode noise rejection; low-Z mics (≤200 Ω) run long distances cleanly.

The Signal Chain

The chain and its levels. Transducers at both ends; the amplifier sits immediately before the loudspeakers.

Chain order

Mic (acoustic→electrical) → mixer (combine + gain) → processor (EQ, dynamics) → amplifier → loudspeakers. "The amplifier comes right before the loudspeakers" is a literal review question.

Gain adjustment

Changing a signal's level. Unity gain = out equals in. Attenuation = reduction. Getting levels right at each stage preserves signal-to-noise.

Crossover

An electrical frequency-dividing network — routes lows to woofers, highs to tweeters. Not the speaker itself; the dividing circuit.

Drivers & systems

A driver is an individual loudspeaker unit. A distributed system uses many loudspeakers to cover an area at constant SPL — versus point-source covering from one location.

Ch. 4 — Most Tested

  • Sound needs a compressible medium; 343 m/s at 20°C sea level; λ = v/f
  • JND = ±3 dB; reverberation = numerous persistent reflections
  • Dynamic = coil/magnet, rugged, no power; condenser = capacitor, needs phantom (12–48 V, safe for dynamics)
  • Ceiling mics = poorest pickup position; wireless needs frequency coordination
  • XLR on shielded twisted pair; low impedance = longer runs, less noise
  • Amp feeds loudspeakers directly; crossover = frequency-dividing circuit

Video and Light

Light measurement, image capture, signal formats, flat panels, and projection — the widest chapter in the book.

Light & Color

FACTVALUEWHAT IT MEANS
Illuminance units1 fc = 10.76 luxLight landing on a surface: lumens/ft² (fc) vs lumens/m² (lux). Meter readings for screen and task lighting use these.
Color temperatureKelvin (K)Warm/orange light ≈ 2700–3200 K; daylight ≈ 5600–6500 K. Mixed color temperatures in one room wreck camera white balance.
Color rendering indexCRI, 0–100How faithfully a light source reveals colors versus a reference source. Higher = truer color — matters for video capture spaces.
Video signal bandwidthV-sync = Hz · H-sync = kHz · picture detail = MHzA video signal spans a frequency range; the fine image detail lives at the top. Bandwidth = the range a circuit can pass — starve it and detail dies first.
Full-bandwidth analogRGBHV = 5 conductorsOne cable each for red, green, blue, horizontal sync, vertical sync (the VGA connector carries exactly this). Encoding trades bandwidth for added noise/artifacts.
Every display technology is one of three light methods. Classify first — the tradeoffs follow from the method.
\n

Displays

Three light methods

Transmissive (light passes through: LCD, rear projection) · reflective (bounces off: front projection) · emissive (elements make their own light: LED, OLED, microLED).

LCD anatomy

Liquid crystals between two polarizers act as light shutters per pixel. Polarizers can't block everything, so LCD "black" is really dark gray. The black matrix shields transistors and causes the screen-door effect; higher aperture ratio = more light through, fewer artifacts.

Emissive family

OLED and microLED emit per-pixel — true blacks, wide angles. Fine-pitch LED tiles are modular, stackable, extremely bright — now dominating large venues, signage, and touring production.

Native resolution

The display's physical pixel grid. Feed it exactly that and no scaling occurs — the sharpest possible image. (EDID is how the display tells the source.)

Projection

3LCD

Light split into R/G/B, each through its own LCD panel, recombined optically. Additive color does the rest.

DLP / DMD

Thousands of MEMS micromirrors tilt to reflect light through the lens (on) or into an absorber (off). Two states only, so grayscale comes from pulse-width modulation. 3DLP = three chips; single-chip = spinning color wheel synced to the DMD.

Light sources

Lamps: tungsten halogen (cheap, dim, short-lived) → metal halide (efficient, long life) → xenon arc (very bright, hazardous pressure). Solid-state (LED / laser-phosphor / RGB laser): tens of thousands of hours, no lamp swaps — the industry direction.

Front vs. rear

Front: less space, cheaper, but the screen reflects ambient light too, the projector's fans share the room, and presenters cast shadows (ultra-short-throw helps). Rear: transmissive, better contrast in ambient light, no shadows or fan noise — but needs a projection room/cabinet (mirrors fold the path).

Screens

Matte white = good color for data/graphics; matte gray = deeper dark-area contrast; angularly reflective (gain > 5) = mirror-like bounce for motion video. Rear diffusion material = wide angles, little gain, possible hot-spotting.

Throw & the inverse square law

Pull the projector back for a bigger image and brightness drops with the square of the distance — the Ch. 5 restatement of the same −6 dB-per-doubling physics.

Keystone

Trapezoidal distortion when the projector isn't perpendicular to the screen. Fix physically first; digital correction discards pixels.

Ch. 5 — Most Tested

  • 1 fc = 10.76 lux; color temp in Kelvin; CRI = color fidelity
  • Transmissive / reflective / emissive — classify any display technology
  • LCD black = dark gray (polarizer leakage); DLP grayscale = PWM on micromirrors
  • RGBHV = 5 signals; picture detail is the high-frequency (MHz) content
  • Front projection: ambient light and shadows; rear: space cost, better contrast
  • Bigger throw = dimmer image (inverse square); native resolution = no scaling
PART II · CHAPTER 6

Networks

Network types, topologies, Ethernet, OSI, addressing, and the hardware roles. AV rides on IT infrastructure now — this is the shared vocabulary.

Network Types & Topologies

ITEMDEFINITIONWHAT IT MEANS
LAN → WAN ladderPAN · LAN/WLAN · CAN · MAN · WAN · SAN · VLANPersonal → local (user-owned, short range) → campus → metro → wide (the Internet is the biggest WAN). SAN = storage; VLAN = a logical group spanning physical LANs.
StarAll nodes to a central switch/routerThe most common topology in general use.
BusOne cable, node to node in a lineSeen in control systems like lighting; each node has a unique ID.
RingBus with the ends joined into a loopThe loop creates a redundant path — traffic survives a single break. Telecom/WAN providers use mesh-ring hybrids.
MeshEvery node linked to at least one otherMany possible paths = high failure resilience. The topology of ad hoc wireless, building automation, and IoT.
EthernetIEEE 802 packet standardData broken into packets ≤ 1,500 bits. 802.11 is the Wi-Fi branch of the same family.

Addressing & the Stack

The OSI stack with the TCP/IP model beside it. Memory anchors: Layer 1 = the cables you pull; Layer 2 = MAC; Layer 3 = IP; Layer 7 = the app.

OSI's 7 layers

1-Physical (cabling, patchbays — where AV lives) · 2-Data Link (MAC) · 3-Network (IP, routing) · 4-Transport · 5-Session · 6-Presentation · 7-Application. TCP/IP compresses the same jobs into 4 layers (5–7 → Application; 1–2 → Network Access).

MAC address

The hardware address burned into every NIC: 48 bits as six hex pairs (01:23:45:67:89:ab). First half identifies the manufacturer, second half is a serial — globally unique per device.

IP addressing

IPv4 = four decimal octets; IPv6 = eight groups of four hex digits. Manual config needs IP + subnet mask minimum; DHCP assigns automatically on join; DNS maps names to addresses.

Subnet mask

Defines which portion of the address is network vs. host — and thereby how many devices the subnet can hold. Subnets isolate broadcast traffic.

The Hardware Roles

Switch

Learns MAC addresses into memory and forwards frames to the right port. Lets each device run at its own max speed. Unmanaged = plug and play; managed = VLANs, QoS, port control, monitoring.

Router

Works a layer above the switch — reads destination IP addresses and moves packets between networks along defined paths. Routers form hierarchies.

Gateway

The topmost router — the door to outside networks. All outbound traffic ultimately passes through it; some devices won't operate without a gateway address set.

Bridge & firewall

A bridge translates one network protocol to another (a broadband modem bridges Ethernet ↔ WAN). A firewall filters traffic against security rules.

Ch. 6 — Most Tested

  • Star = most common; ring = built-in redundancy; mesh = IoT/ad hoc wireless; bus = sequential single cable
  • OSI Layer 1 = cabling/patchbays; MAC = Layer 2 hardware ID; IP/routing = Layer 3
  • MAC = 48-bit six hex pairs; IPv6 = 8 groups of 4 hex; manual IP = address + subnet mask; DHCP automates it
  • Switch learns MACs; router reads IPs; gateway = topmost router; bridge = protocol translator
  • Wi-Fi = 802.11; weaker signal = slower connection, not a dropped one
PART II · CHAPTER 7

Signal-Management Systems

Cable anatomy, signal integrity, switchers, distribution amps, and the rack they all live in.

Cable & Signal Integrity

Cable anatomy

Four layers: conductor (the signal path) → insulation → shield (foil or braid intercepting interference) → jacket (physical protection). Coax = unbalanced by construction; twisted pair + balanced circuit = noise rejection; fiber = immune to EMI entirely.

Bundle by level

Mic-level with mic-level, line with line — never mixed. And route away from noise sources: motors, fluorescent or dimmed lighting, power distribution, battery chargers, transmitters. Degradation shows as amplitude loss, waveform distortion, or phase shift.

Distance limits

Governed by signal level, bandwidth, and cable loss. Digital signals travel shorter distances than analog without buffering or amplification — counterintuitive but true. Consult standards + manufacturer specs.

Switching & Distribution

Inside a matrix switcher: distribution amps on every input, a crosspoint grid, one switcher's worth of logic per output. Filled dots = active routes.

Switcher vs. mixer

A switcher sends one selected input to a destination; a mixer combines several at once. Passive switchers need no power; active ones add buffering, gain, and EQ per input.

Matrix switcher

Any input → any/many outputs simultaneously. Internally it's one switcher per output, with a DA on each input feeding all of them. A 4×2 matrix = two 4-input switchers.

Crash vs. seamless

A mechanical ("crash") switcher ignores signal content — video jumps and rolls mid-frame. A seamless switcher buffers, watches sync, and cuts in the interval between frames. Use seamless anywhere image quality matters.

Distribution amplifier

An amplifier with a gain of one — it doesn't boost, it isolates: one input to many outputs at the original level. Classic case: one lectern mic feeding 24 isolated press feeds; one shorted press cable affects only that feed.

Rack Numbers

SPECVALUEWHAT IT MEANS
Interior width19 in (482.6 mm)The universal standard mounting width; exterior runs 21–25 in (533–635 mm).
Rack unit1 RU = 1.75 in (44.45 mm)The vertical currency of every rack elevation drawing. Racks range ~6 RU to 48 RU (7 ft).

Ch. 7 — Most Tested

  • Conductor → insulation → shield → jacket; bundle cables by signal level
  • Digital travels shorter than analog without buffering
  • DA = gain of one, isolation + distribution, not amplification
  • Matrix = any-in to any-out with internal DAs; seamless switches between frames, crash switchers roll the picture
  • 19-in rack, 1 RU = 1.75 in / 44.45 mm
PART II · CHAPTER 8

Control Systems

The whole purpose of a control system is to simplify AV operation. Six control types, plus the function/program distinction.

KEY TERM Function = a single action on one device (set a volume level, turn on lights). Program = a sequence of functions fired together ("start presentation" = dim lights + power display + amp on). Interfaces (touch panels, wall switches, GUIs) send instructions to the control system CPU, which executes them.
The control loop. Bidirectional communication returns status — the user always knows what the device did. Unidirectional sends and hopes.
\n

The Six Control Types

TYPEHOW IT WORKSWHAT IT MEANS
Contact closureCircuit open or closed — pure on/offThe simplest protocol. Drives screens, drapes, shades, motors via a relay; also works as an input: occupancy sensors, pressure mats, partition-position switches.
Variable voltageAnalog level within a range0–10 V DC dimming is the canonical case: 5 V = 50% output. A legacy analog method (also ramp voltage for lens/PTZ control).
Infrared (IR)Pulses of invisible light, LED → phototransistorRequires line of sight; defeated by bright sunshine and fluorescent lighting. Cheap, ubiquitous, unidirectional.
Serial digitalRS-232 / RS-422 / RS-485RS-232: DB-9, unbalanced, bidirectional, short runs. RS-422: balanced → longer distances. RS-485: balanced, 32 devices, 120 Ω terminations (DMX512's electrical layer).
RFRadio linkNo line of sight needed — but susceptible to interference, so test on site. Mostly used for user interfaces (remotes, panels).
NetworkedTCP/IP over EthernetThe method for complex systems. Every device needs a unique IP or DNS hostname — coordinated with the client's IT department.

Bidirectional = feedback

Device executes the command and reports back — the user always knows device status. Unidirectional sends and hopes. Bidirectional is preferred wherever available.

The IT handoff list

For networked control, give the IT manager: device, manufacturer, model, MAC address, firmware version, location, why it needs the network, and bandwidth — then let them assign IPs/hostnames. Early coordination prevents late surprises.

Ch. 8 — Most Tested

  • Control systems exist to simplify operation; interfaces talk to the CPU
  • Function = single action; program = sequence (powering amp + display = program)
  • Contact closure = open/close a contact; variable voltage = analog level (0–10 V dimming)
  • IR fails in sunlight/fluorescent light and needs line of sight
  • Bidirectional = feedback/status; Ethernet control = complex systems
PART II · CHAPTER 9

Electrical Systems

Ohm's law, how power reaches the outlet, and the grounding rules that keep people alive and signals clean.

Electrical Basics

The quantities

Voltage (V) = potential difference, the push. Current (I, amperes) = electron flow — always seeking to return to its source. Resistance/impedance (Ω) opposes flow. Power (watts) = the work done.

Ohm's law family

V = I × R, so I = V/R and R = V/I. Power: P = V × I. Two knowns give you everything else — the exam tests recognition of the rearrangements.

Series vs. parallel

Series: one path, same current everywhere, voltage divides across resistances (biggest R drops most V). Parallel: same voltage across every branch, current divides (biggest R draws least I).

Power distribution path

Utility → main panel → feeders → distribution panels (panelboards) → branch circuits → wall outlets and AV gear. Know the order.

The two circuit configurations and their one-liner rules. Everything else derives from Ohm's law applied to each.
\n

Grounding & Safety

Safety ground (earthing)

A low-resistance path that gives fault current a fast way home, tripping the breaker. This is life-safety infrastructure — never defeat it, never use ground-lift adapters.

GFCI / RCD

Senses tiny leakage current to ground and cuts the supply in under 1/10 second. Code-mandated in wet and many temporary-power contexts.

Isolated ground & technical power

Gives all AV equipment one clean ground reference, killing ground loops — heard as 50/60 Hz hum, seen as rolling bars. Technical power circuits are for AV gear only: no coffee makers, no vacuums.

Class II equipment

Double-insulated — the square-inside-a-square symbol — needs no ground conductor at all.

The 80% rule

Never plan any circuit, fixed or temporary, beyond 80% of its rated current capacity.

Ch. 9 — Most Tested

  • I = V/R and its rearrangements; power in watts; current returns to source
  • Main panel → feeders → distribution panels → branch circuits
  • 80% max planned circuit load
  • GFCI trips in < 1/10 s on ground leakage
  • Ground loops = hum + rolling bars → isolated grounding, never ground lifts
PART II · CHAPTER 10

Wireless Radio Systems

Short chapter, precise vocabulary. The radio/RF/wireless distinction alone is worth points.

WATCH OUT Three terms that are not interchangeable: Radio waves = electromagnetic radiation, 3 kHz–300 GHz. RF = any signal in that frequency range — it can travel in a copper cable (your set-top box feed is RF but not radio). Wireless = any connection without wire or fiber — including IR remotes, which use no radio at all.

Transmit & Receive

Modulation

The transmitter's essential act: impressing the information onto an RF carrier (the "envelope"). The carrier frequency must be at least 2× the information frequency. The receiver demodulates to recover the original. Modulation is "the most important step."

Propagation

Radio travels in straight lines — line of sight is ideal. Longer wavelengths penetrate walls better and bounce off the ionosphere; very short wavelengths pass through it (satellites) but rain and even clouds can block them.

Antenna sizing

A highly efficient vertical antenna is ¼ of the signal's wavelength. Parabolic (dish) antennas suit point-to-point links. Quick math: wavelength in meters = 300 ÷ frequency in MHz (100 MHz → 3 m).

Diversity receivers

Multiple antennas receiving the same transmission; the receiver compares phase differences and picks/combines antennas to dodge multipath cancellation — the defense against wireless-mic dropouts.

Modulation, the transmitter's essential act — the information shapes the carrier's envelope (shown: amplitude modulation). Demodulation reverses it.
\n

Gathering Customer Information

The most padded chapter in the book — but the program-phase concepts it introduces anchor everything through Ch. 16.

The program phase, start to finish. Everything feeds the report; the approved report feeds the design.

Needs analysis

Identifies what end users need to do — the top of the pyramid. Best source: end users describing their tasks. Systems built on designer assumptions are "frequently off the mark." Never starts with equipment.

Benchmarking

Letting the client experience existing systems that solve similar problems — calibrates expectations before anything is drawn.

Program report

AKA concept design report, return brief, AV narrative. Contents: executive summary, space planning, systems descriptions, infrastructure, AV budgets. Audience: decision-makers, users, design team, and eventually the integrator — sometimes it becomes the RFP.

Documentation to collect

Architectural drawings (as-builts or in progress), engineering drawings (power, conduit, HVAC noise), project directories, contract scopes, owner/end-user info. Send detailed technical info to clients as an electronic document.

Ch. 11 — Most Tested

  • Best information source = end users; include them so all parties are invested

Conducting a Site Survey

Reading architectural drawings, scale math, and unit conversion — the most mechanically testable chapter in Part III.

The four core views, plus the two specialists. Exam questions hand you a task and ask which drawing answers it.

Scale & Conversion Math

real size = measured size × scale factor1:50 scale, you measure 20 cm → 20 × 50 = 1,000 cm = 10 m real-world

Site survey checklist

Captures general client and site information relevant to design and installation — access, power, environment, contacts. PPE requirements on site are set by the AHJ (authority having jurisdiction), plus client and company rules.

PART III · CHAPTER 13

Evaluating a Site Environment

Judging the room itself: viewing, lighting, acoustics, and sightlines — the chapter where DISCAS viewing tasks live.

Sightline geometry from the worst seat. If the amber line hits a head, the design fails before any equipment is chosen.

Viewing tasks (DISCAS)

Passive (movies, signage) · basic decision-making (read slides, act on them — sized by smallest text element) · analytical decision-making (pixel-level: medical imaging, CAD, forensics) · full-motion (detail while moving). The task category selects the sizing calculation.

Ambient light rules contrast

A front screen reflects all light, projected and ambient alike — black can never be darker than the unlit screen, so ambient light sets the contrast floor. Windows facing screens are enemy #1.

Room features to record

Doors and swing paths, windows and treatments, interior finishes (light surfaces near the screen bounce light into it), ceiling type and height, cable access locations, and the technical functions of any control/projection areas.

Acoustics

Background noise quantified by NC / NR / RC ratings; reverberation from hard parallel surfaces; HVAC diffusers and projector fans are the classic noise sources. Speech needs level and uniformity at every seat.

Ch. 13 — Most Tested

  • Match the space to the viewing task (radiology room = ADM), the task to the sizing rule
  • Front screens reflect everything; ambient light sets the contrast floor
  • Sightline study elements: lowest visible screen point, near/far viewers, off-axis angles
  • NC/NR/RC = background noise ratings; finishes near the screen should be dark and neutral
PART III · CHAPTER 14

Recommending Site Changes

Short chapter: the evaluation from Ch. 13 becomes recommendations — lighting, acoustics, finishes, and HVAC.

The minimum lighting design: two independently controlled zones, dark finishes near the screen, windows tamed or eliminated from the screen wall.

Lighting recommendations

Minimum two zones, separately switched/dimmed. Kill light on the screen; keep enough on the audience. Recommend treatments (shades, blackout) for windows — and never place a screen opposite one.

Acoustic recommendations

Treatment for hard parallel surfaces, isolation or rescheduling of noisy HVAC, and attention to noise ratings from Ch. 13. The goal is hitting the background-noise target the design assumes (≤ 45 dB SPL A for conference speech).

Finish & furniture recommendations

Darker, neutral, non-reflective surfaces near the screen; furniture and layout that respect sightlines and mic placement.

The coordination reality

These changes belong to other trades — architects, electrical, mechanical. The AV professional recommends, documents the reasoning, and coordinates; the program/design documents carry the requirement.

Ch. 14 — Most Tested

  • Two lighting zones minimum, independently controlled
  • Screen-facing windows: treat, relocate the screen, or both
  • Dark neutral finishes near the display; acoustic treatment for reflective parallel surfaces
  • AV recommends and coordinates — the changes execute through other trades
PART III · CHAPTER 15

Developing an AV Project Scope

Scope development ≈ 9% of the exam. The documents ARE the content here — know what each one is for and when it exists.

The document chain. Exam questions test which document holds which information at which phase.

Scope of work

Defines what the project includes — and by exclusion, what it doesn't. Drawn from the approved program report. Ambiguity here is where projects bleed money.

Design documentation

Drawings + written specs conveying fabrication/installation intent, system functions, and required quality. Competitive bidding demands detailed written specifications, conforming to the client's and jurisdiction's construction-documentation standards.

Drawing set contents

Functional (block/flow) diagrams, system drawings per subsystem, rack elevations, plate/panel details, specialty wiring diagrams, custom configurations (DIP switches, jumpers), patch-panel layouts, user-interface descriptions.

Engineering drawings

The other trades' documents — mechanical, electrical, structural, plumbing. The AV designer reads them for power, conduit, data infrastructure, and HVAC noise flags.

Display system drives the room

Design the viewing experience first (DISCAS: task → size → viewer positions), then fit everything else around it. Every path component needs adequate bandwidth and latency.

DSP toolbox

Equalizers (shape response to the application — paging needs no bass; expect a preferred equalization curve from the designer), echo cancelers (electronic = line echo; acoustic = far-site sound bouncing back into mics), gates, compressors, limiters/AGC, matrix mixing.

Blank sheet: devices needing control on the left, required functions per device on the right. That function list is the programmer's brief; then interoperability and interconnectivity.

PART IV · CHAPTER 17

Integrating AV Solutions

The hands-on chapter: terminations, cable pulling, racks, mounting, and control/network configuration.

The four termination methods and their signature connectors. The multimeter (or 8P8C tester) is the verdict.

Cable Pulling Rules

Sequence with trades

Plenum cable goes in after electrical, ducts, and ceiling supports — before ceiling tiles. Electronics come only after the site is clean and dust-free.

Pull discipline

Pull from spools (don't pre-cut), keep spools upright on holders, feed straight, no twisting, don't cross cables entering conduit, don't pull fast, leave slack at pull boxes, constant radio contact between both ends. Jammed? Pull back toward the spool.

Crosstalk geometry

When cable paths must cross, cross at 90° to minimize coupling. Low-level signals take the most direct route — they're the most vulnerable to interference and loss.

Label everything

Color-coding speeds bundle identification but never replaces labeling. Add extra length for termination and error.

Racks, Mounting, Config

Rack layout logic

Heat, signal separation, user access, weight distribution — all decided on the rack elevation drawing before assembly. Heavy low; hot high or spaced; fill every RU; user gear in the 380–1,220 mm reach band; record serials before loading.

RF/IR in metal boxes

The rack shields rack-mounted wireless receivers from their own antennas — position antennas outside the shadow.

Mounting is life-safety

Displays can top 50 kg (110 lb) and hang over people. Structure, hardware ratings, and manufacturer instructions are non-negotiable; malfunctions here injure people, not just budgets.

Control & network config

Terminate control wiring (RS-232/422/485 rules from Ch. 8), load and test control software, and configure networked AV to the IT-coordinated plan — device list with MACs to the IT manager, addresses/hostnames back.

Ch. 17 — Most Tested

  • Termination method ↔ connector: solder–XLR/phone · crimp · linear compression–BNC · insulation displacement–8P8C
  • Test terminations immediately; stray wires = fabrication failure
  • Cross cables at 90°; low-level signals take the direct route
  • Color coding ≠ labeling; pull from spools; AV electronics installed after trades finish, into a clean site
  • Rack: heavy low, hot high, fill every space, signal separation

PART IV · CHAPTER 19

Project Management

≈ 8% of the exam. The WBS → logic network → Gantt chain (diagrammed in the Part doc) plus the money-and-change vocabulary.

The two change instruments. The exam's favorite trap is swapping them — match the driver to the document.

The planning chain

WBS = nouns, what the client receives (no who/when/cost) → logic network = verbs, sequenced by dependency, milestones as diamonds → Gantt = the calendar with dependency links. See the full diagram in the Part essentials doc.

Effort vs. duration

Effort = perfect-world work time. Duration = calendar reality — resource availability, competing projects, holidays. Estimating with effort alone underbids every job.

Price ≠ cost

Client price = labor/materials cost + markup covering overhead (rent, insurance, benefits) and profit. Budget = the labor + materials allocated to the agreed work.

Dependency

A task that can't start until another finishes — mount the display only after the wall is painted. Dependencies are what the logic network exists to capture.

Ch. 19 — Most Tested

  • WBS = deliverables/nouns · logic network = activities/dependencies · Gantt = timeline
  • Change order: signed by both parties, before out-of-scope work
  • RFC: the AV company's document when building changes affect AV
  • Effort ≠ duration; price = cost + markup (overhead + profit)

PART V · CHAPTER 21

Maintaining and Repairing AV Solutions

The warranty taxonomy, preventive maintenance, and the test-equipment shelf.

Who pays for what, when. Early failure = manufacturer; contracted ongoing care = service agreement.

Preventive maintenance

Replace limited-life components before failure on a tracked schedule (lamps by rated hours, filters), recalibrate drifted or user-mangled settings, and physically re-check mounts and positions. Visits are scheduled around the client's operations and defined in the agreement.

First response to a report

Gather symptom information before traveling: working at all? powers on? error messages? all sources or one? It narrows causes and decides what goes in the van. If it can't be fixed on site, a loaner may keep the client running per the agreement.

The test bench

Multimeter, oscilloscope, waveform monitor, sound-level meter, cable testers, impedance meter, audio-signal & video-pattern generators, spectrum analyzer, light meter, DMX512 tester, clamp ammeter, non-contact voltage detector, laptop + adapters, network testers.

Service records

Every visit logged: technician, date/time, work performed, downtime, user reports, follow-ups. Any system change updates the as-builts and manuals. A PM visit ends with a findings report to the client.

Ch. 21 — Most Tested

  • Manufacturer's warranty = early failures · system warranty = integrator's term · service agreement = contracted ongoing PM
  • Preventive maintenance = scheduled replacement before failure (the lamp example)
  • First step on any problem report: gather symptom detail
  • Software/firmware updates can cascade — evaluate before installing, even on owner equipment
PART V · CHAPTER 22

Troubleshooting and Repairing

≈ 10% of the exam, and nearly all of it is one method plus four named strategies.

Binary search on a signal path. Red = where the fault actually lives; each amber test halves the suspects.

The four named strategies

Swapping (known-good replaces suspect — but only rules out that one part) · divide and conquer (halve, test, repeat) · divide into subsystems (audio vs. video vs. control first, then halve within) · signal flow (walk the path end to end from either direction).

The mic scenario

"Mic doesn't work": muted channel? fader down? condenser without phantom on? right wall-plate input? right mixer channel? mic/line switch? Then swap a known-good mic — and remember swapping only clears the mic itself.

Network diagnosis

Ping and companion tools verify reachability before anyone blames the AV gear — Layer 1 and addressing problems masquerade as device failures constantly.

Close the loop

Repair or work-around → verify full operation → document the fix → record labor and parts for accurate billing. An undocumented repair is a future troubleshooting session.

Ch. 22 — Most Tested

  • First step, always: clearly identify the failure symptoms
  • Most efficient isolation: divide the system in half, repeat
  • Most common failure point: cables and connectors; swapping clears only the swapped part
  • Intermittent = difficult to reproduce; always consider user error
  • "What's the NEXT logical step?" questions follow the method — wrong answers skip or regress
